You can monetize YouTube Shorts, and YouTube does not publish a fixed dollars-per-thousand-views rate for doing it. Eligible creators earn inside the YouTube Partner Program. Ads run between videos in the Shorts Feed, that revenue is pooled, music licensing can be funded from the pool, and monetizing creators keep 45% of the revenue allocated to them. YouTube lists two full paths: 1,000 subscribers plus 4,000 qualified public watch hours in the last 12 months, or 1,000 subscribers plus 10 million qualified public Shorts views in the last 90 days. Watch time from the Shorts Feed does not count toward the 4,000-hour path. Rules YouTube has dated February 1, 2027 are not the same sentence as today's application thresholds, so read [YouTube's Shorts monetization policies](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/12504220?hl=en) and confirm the channel in YouTube Studio before you quote earnings.

## Can you make money on YouTube Shorts?

Yes. A creator who is in the YouTube Partner Program, and who has accepted the Shorts Monetization Module, can earn from ads viewed between videos in the Shorts Feed and from YouTube Premium revenue that YouTube allocates to Shorts. Seeing an ad on a Short is not the same event. YouTube's Partner Program overview says YouTube may serve ads on content even when the channel is not eligible to receive a share. The useful question is narrower: is the channel eligible, has the right module been accepted, and are the views on that Short eligible.

Shorts ad revenue is also a poor single plan for a business. It is one official path. Long-form watch time, affiliate offers, sponsorships, products, and fan-funding features can sit beside it. None of those paths has a rate you can copy from a stranger's screenshot.

## What has to be true before YouTube reviews the channel?

The YouTube Partner Program is not a switch that flips when a view counter hits a round number. [YouTube's eligibility overview](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/72851?hl=en) says a channel needs all of the following before it can apply:

- The channel follows [YouTube channel monetization policies](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1311392?hl=en).
- The channel owner lives in a country or region where the Partner Program is available.
- The channel has no active Community Guidelines strikes.
- 2-Step Verification is on for the Google Account.
- The channel has advanced features access.
- An active AdSense for YouTube account is linked, or the creator is ready to create one inside YouTube Studio.

Meeting a view threshold does not skip the review. YouTube says every channel that crosses a threshold goes through a review of the channel as a whole. YouTube describes the decision as typically taking about a month, with delays possible. If a first application is rejected, YouTube says you can appeal within 21 days, or reapply after 30 days. If the rejection is not the first, or you have already reapplied, YouTube says the wait is 90 days. A channel that has not uploaded a video or posted to the Posts tab for 6 months or more can have monetization turned off. Those clocks are reasons to keep original uploads moving, not reasons to flood the channel with reused clips just to look active.

If subscriber or watch-hour counts fall below the threshold after you have applied, YouTube says the review can still proceed. Changing a video to private, or deleting a video, can affect the application. Check the Earn tab rather than guessing from a public subscriber count.

## What are the two full YouTube Partner Program paths?

YouTube states two ways to become eligible for the full Partner Program:

| Path | What YouTube requires | What does not count |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Long-form | 1,000 subscribers and 4,000 qualified public watch hours in the last 12 months | Shorts Feed watch time, private videos, unlisted videos, deleted videos, ad campaigns, and livestreams that stay unlisted, are deleted, or are not converted to a video on demand |
| Shorts | 1,000 subscribers and 10 million qualified public Shorts views in the last 90 days | Private, unlisted, or deleted Shorts, ad campaigns, and image posts in the Shorts Feed |

A Shorts-first channel should track the Shorts path on its own. YouTube is explicit that qualified watch hours from Shorts views in the Shorts Feed do not count toward the 4,000-hour threshold. A long-form video and a Short can support the same topic without sharing the same eligibility math.

Qualified Shorts views, for the threshold, are views of public Shorts that appear in the Shorts Feed. That is a different sentence from "every public play on the internet." A number in a social screenshot is not a qualified-view report. YouTube Studio is the place that separates the two.

## How does YouTube share Shorts ad revenue?

Shorts monetization is not long-form monetization with a vertical crop. On the Watch page, an ad can be tied to one video. In the Shorts Feed, YouTube pools revenue from ads that play between videos, then distributes that pool. [YouTube's Shorts monetization policies](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/12504220?hl=en) describe four steps:

1. Revenue from ads running between videos in the Shorts Feed is added up for the month. That money both rewards creators and helps cover music licensing.
2. YouTube calculates the Creator Pool from engaged views and from whether monetizing creators used music.
3. YouTube allocates the Creator Pool to monetizing creators by their share of eligible engaged Shorts views in each country. YouTube's own illustration: a creator with 5% of those views is allocated 5% of the Creator Pool.
4. The creator keeps 45% of that allocation. YouTube says this 45% applies whether or not the Short used music.

Only partners who have accepted the Shorts Monetization Module can earn this revenue. YouTube says sharing applies to eligible Shorts views from the date you accept. Views from before that acceptance are not eligible. If you have not accepted the module, ads can still run, and YouTube says the remaining revenue covers music licensing or stays with YouTube.

YouTube also says these amounts stay out of the Creator Pool: views on Shorts from creators who have not accepted the module or are not eligible; views on Shorts uploaded by music partners; views YouTube treats as ineligible; ads shown when someone opens the Shorts Feed before a Short plays, including what YouTube calls the YouTube Shorts Masthead; ads on navigational pages inside the Shorts player; and revenue tied to image posts in the Shorts Feed.

YouTube Analytics can show estimated daily Shorts Feed ad revenue once that revenue is available, or from the day you start monetizing with Shorts ads. Payment timing still follows AdSense for YouTube. This page does not restate an AdSense payout minimum, because the Shorts policy page points at that threshold without quoting it here.

YouTube Premium, including Premium Lite, is a second slice. YouTube says it pays 45% of the net Premium revenue allocated to monetizing creators for Shorts. A portion of Premium revenue is allocated to music licensing. Each creator's payment depends on that creator's share of subscription Shorts views inside each country. Premium revenue is not a second copy of the Feed-ad pool, and it is not a reason to add the two 45% figures together into a made-up rate.

## How does music change the pool without changing the 45%?

Music is the rule people misquote. YouTube says monetizing creators keep 45% of allocated Shorts revenue whether or not music was used. Music changes how much revenue enters the Creator Pool before that split. It is not accurate to say YouTube subtracts a licensing fee from one creator's 45% after the allocation.

YouTube's published examples, on the same policy page, are specific:

| Music in the Short | What enters the Creator Pool from that Short's associated revenue | What covers music licensing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| No music | All of the revenue associated with it | Nothing from this music split |
| 1 track | Half | Half |
| 2 tracks | One third | Two thirds |

YouTube defines "music" here as content made available or claimed by its music partners. That can include a track, a music video, or other music content such as an artist interview, and it includes music generated by Dream Track. YouTube also says that, when the pool amount is calculated, only that music is credited as a contribution. Other third-party content is not, for now, credited the same way, even if a Content ID monetize policy is set. When YouTube pays creators out of the pool, it says each monetizing creator is allocated 100% of the engaged views on their Shorts, music or not. Using music therefore does not change that creator's share of the pool or the 45% rate. Rights problems are a separate issue: a Short can still be blocked, or fall outside advertiser-friendly rules, even when the revenue math would have been fine. For what you can post, use [the copyrighted-music guide](/blog/can-you-use-copyrighted-music-on-youtube-shorts) rather than treating the pool split as permission.

YouTube prints a hypothetical so the steps are visible. It is not a rate card. The example assumes the creator already meets the monthly bar of 10 million qualified Shorts views in the last 90 days, and it looks at one country:

- Country A has 100 million engaged Shorts views, all on Shorts uploaded by monetizing creators.
- Ads between Shorts earn $100,000.
- YouTube's example says 20% of these Shorts use 1 track, so the Creator Pool is $90,000 and $10,000 covers music licensing. That matches the one-track rule: half of the revenue tied to that 20% is 10% of $100,000.
- The creator's Short has 1 million engaged views, which is 1% of 100 million, so the allocation is 1% of $90,000, or $900. YouTube says that allocation is not reduced because the Short used a track.
- Then 45% of $900 is $405. YouTube's example stops at $405 for that creator's Shorts views in Country A for the month.

Change the country mix, the music mix, or the creator's share of views, and the dollars change. Do not divide $405 by 1 million and publish the result as a Shorts RPM. The example exists to show the order of operations.

## Which Shorts views are ineligible or blocked?

YouTube pays on eligible engaged views. The Shorts policy page gives examples of views that do not qualify:

- Non-original Shorts, including unedited clips from someone else's film or TV show, reuploads of another creator's YouTube or off-platform video, and compilations that add no original content.
- Artificial or fake views, including automated click or scroll bots.
- Views of Shorts that break advertiser-friendly content guidelines. Only views of Shorts that follow those guidelines are eligible for revenue sharing.

A separate block sits on top of that list. YouTube says Shorts over one minute that contain claimed content are blocked and are not eligible for monetization. On length and monetization, the same page says a three-minute Short uploaded after October 15, 2024 can monetize in the Shorts revenue-sharing model, and a three-minute video uploaded before that date needs an active first-party claim if you want it monetized as a Short. Those sentences are about eligibility, not a promise that every short vertical upload is a Short. Check the current length rules in Studio if a video is near the boundary. This page does not restate a general maximum.

Reused and repetitive content is a channel-level risk, not only a one-video risk. YouTube requires monetizing channels to follow channel monetization policies, including the rules on repetitious and reused content, plus Community Guidelines, Terms of Service, copyright rules, and Google AdSense program policies. A channel that looks like a pile of other people's clips can fail review even when one original Short is doing fine.

## What has YouTube dated to February 1, 2027?

Treat February 1, 2027 as a dated change, and read it on YouTube's pages rather than in a recap that flattens it into today's rules. Two statements on the pages this guide uses are easy to merge by mistake.

The Shorts monetization page says that, to be eligible for Shorts ad revenue and YouTube Premium revenue for each month, you need at least 10 million qualified Shorts views in the last 90 days. The same page says that starting February 1, 2027, eligibility to earn from the Creator Pool and from YouTube Premium each month uses that 10 million figure, measured over the 90-day period that ends on the 15th day of the calendar month before the month being paid. The 15th-day measurement method is part of the dated sentence. Do not invent a payout for a month YouTube has not calculated.

Starting the same day, YouTube says Shorts ads targeted to specific channels in groups of 5 or fewer channels can earn direct revenue. For those ads, creators keep 45% of the net revenue from the specific ad, in addition to whatever the Creator Pool pays from ordinary Shorts Feed ads. That is a second 45% sentence. It is not a new rate on every Short, and it does not apply to ads that are not in that targeted group.

The Partner Program overview says YouTube is introducing YPP updates on February 1, 2027, and that creators who want to keep fully monetizing should review and accept the updated terms in YouTube Studio by January 31, 2027. This guide does not summarize terms that live only on a page those two Help articles do not quote. Open Studio. If the module is waiting, the article you read last week is not the acceptance.

## Which revenue paths sit next to Shorts ads?

Shorts ad sharing pays when the channel is in the program and the views are eligible. It does not, by itself, tell you whether the viewer wanted a product, a longer video, or nothing. A practical mix uses Shorts as discovery and puts the offer where the format can hold it:

| Path | When it fits | What you still have to prove |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Shorts Feed ad sharing | You are in YPP, you accepted the Shorts module, and you publish eligible Shorts | Eligible engaged views, not a public view count |
| YouTube Premium allocation | The same monthly eligibility YouTube states for Shorts subscription revenue | A share of subscription Shorts views by country, not a guessed add-on |
| Long-form video | The Short introduces a topic people will watch for much longer on the Watch page | Qualified watch hours on public long-form, tracked separately from Shorts |
| Affiliate offer | You can show the product in use and disclose the relationship | Clicks and disclosed context, not a borrowed conversion rate |
| Sponsorship | The channel has a clear topic, brand-safe videos, and an audience a sponsor can recognize | Your own retention and audience notes, not a category rate card |
| Product or service | The Short demonstrates a problem you can solve again | A path off the Short that you control |
| Fan funding, Memberships, Supers, and commerce features | You have accepted the module YouTube requires for that feature | The feature is on for your country and your channel, which Studio shows and a blog post cannot |

Do not publish a sponsorship rate, an affiliate commission, or an RPM as if YouTube set it. Those numbers move with niche, country, season, and whether the viewer was a real prospect. If you cannot separate revenue by stream inside Studio, you do not yet have a monetization report. You have a view count. [YouTube Shorts analytics](/blog/youtube-shorts-analytics) is the worksheet for the fields you do own. Growing into either eligibility path is a different job from pricing a sponsor, and [growing a Shorts channel](/blog/how-to-grow-youtube-shorts-channel) is about the audience, not about a payout formula.

## How should you decide what is worth monetizing?

For each Short you hope will earn, record the questions Studio can actually answer:

- Eligible Shorts views, kept separate from total views.
- View duration and the shape of retention on your own video. Another channel's retention graph is not available to you.
- Returning viewers and subscribers gained from the Short, where Studio shows them.
- Comments that mention a product, a problem, or a sponsor-shaped question.
- Clicks from the Short to a long-form video, a product page, or a link you placed on purpose.
- Revenue by stream, after the module is on, kept apart from likes.

A Short with a large public view count and no eligible views is a distribution event, not a revenue event. A Short with modest views and a clear next step can be the better business asset. Compare a Short with your own similar Shorts over the same number of days. A single spike does not name its cause.

It does not show another creator's revenue, eligible-view status, or private retention. Tracking in ContHunt is experimental, and it is still public data. None of that replaces the Earn tab.

## What should you check before you quote a number?

Use this list before a tweet, a media kit, or a sponsor email mentions Shorts money:

- The channel is in YPP, or you can say which official path it has not reached.
- The Shorts Monetization Module is accepted, and you are not counting views from before that date.
- The Short is original, advertiser-friendly, and clear on music and other rights.
- You are quoting eligible views, or you are explicitly quoting a public view count and not calling it revenue.
- You have at least one path besides Feed ads: long-form, affiliate, sponsor, product, or fan funding.
- Any dollar example you repeat is labeled as YouTube's hypothetical, or as your own Studio figure, and not as a niche benchmark.
- You reread the Shorts monetization page and the Partner Program page if the claim will still be online after February 1, 2027.

YouTube Shorts monetization is a revenue-share program with a published creator share of 45% of allocated revenue, plus an audience you can point somewhere else. The thresholds, the music split, and the dated 2027 rules are all checkable on YouTube's own pages. Anything more precise than that belongs in your Studio account, not in a borrowed rate.
