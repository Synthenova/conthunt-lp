BuzzSumo pricing is a page you read on BuzzSumo's site, not a number this article should freeze. Written by the ContHunt team. Open the current [BuzzSumo pricing page](https://buzzsumo.com/pricing/). Write down the plan, the seats, and the source coverage. Write down the alert limits, the export limits, and whether influencer, PR, or monitoring features are included. Then decide which research job you are paying for. BuzzSumo is built for broad web content, PR, influencer, and historical content research. That job is valuable, and it is a different job from daily research on public TikTok videos, Instagram Reels, and YouTube Shorts. ContHunt is relevant when the daily work is those public videos. It is not a one-for-one replacement for BuzzSumo's web monitoring and PR database.

This page does not quote a BuzzSumo dollar figure, a historical free tier, or a trial length. Those change, and the honest comparison starts by refusing to invent them.

## What is BuzzSumo priced to do?

BuzzSumo is priced as a broad content-intelligence database. It covers what is being published on the web and what is being shared. On the plans that include them, it also covers PR and influencer workflows around that content. You are not buying "a social media tool" in the abstract. You are buying coverage of a source list, a history, alerts, exports, and seats.

That framing decides the value question. A team can put a real workload on that database. That week is finding journalists, comparing which web articles traveled, and watching a topic across the open web. A team that spends its week briefing short-form hooks is paying for a database and then doing the actual job in another tab. The second team does not have a BuzzSumo problem. It has a job-match problem.

The official pricing page is the source of truth for what is in each plan this week. A blog post, including this one, is the wrong place to copy a price from memory. If a sales page and an old comparison disagree, the sales page wins. Re-read the old comparison as a method rather than as a quote.

## How do you record a BuzzSumo plan before you compare it?

Record the plan in a fixed set of fields so two vendors can be compared on the same questions. Do this for BuzzSumo and for every alternative. A cheaper tool is not cheaper if it lacks the source, the export, or the alert you use every week.

Write these down from the live page, not from a screenshot in a slide that is a year old.

- Plan name, exactly as the page labels it.
- Price as displayed, including whether the figure is monthly, yearly, or contact sales.
- Seats or users included, and what an extra seat costs if the page says.
- Sources covered: web, news, YouTube, and any social networks the plan actually lists.
- Alert limits and how overage works, if the page explains overage.
- Export limits, and which fields the export contains.
- Whether influencer data, a PR database, or monitoring is included or sold beside the plan.
- Trial or free-tier rules, only if the page offers them today. Do not assume a free tier you remember from an older year is still there.

If a field is missing on the page, write "not stated." Do not fill it with a number from a review site. "Not stated" is a finding. It means you cannot yet claim that the plan does that thing.

An illustrative blank, not a real BuzzSumo plan, shows the shape of the note:

| Field | What you write | Illustrative entry, not a quote |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Plan name | The label on the page | "Not a real plan name" |
| Price | The figure and the billing period | "Reopen the pricing page" |
| Seats | The included count | "Not stated until you read it" |
| Alerts | The limit the page prints | "Copy the limit, do not invent one" |
| Exports | The limit and the fields | "Copy the limit, do not invent one" |
| PR or influencer | Included, add-on, or absent | "Absent until the page says included" |

The right-hand column is deliberately empty of dollars and limits. Inventing a limit to make the table look finished is how a team walks into a contract that does not have that limit.

## Which research job points to which tool?

Match the tool to the question you ask every week. The right-hand column is a starting point, not a score and not a rank.

| Research job | Better starting point |
| --- | --- |
| Broad web content research | BuzzSumo, or another web-content intelligence suite |
| PR monitoring and journalist or influencer workflows | BuzzSumo, or a PR-focused database |
| Search-demand validation | Google Trends, Search Console, or an SEO suite |
| Where an audience pays attention | SparkToro |
| Topics that are gaining attention | Exploding Topics, or Google Trends |
| Public short-form creative research | ContHunt, or another social and video research workflow |
| How your own channel performed | Native platform analytics first |

These tools do not answer one question in different skins. [SparkToro](https://sparktoro.com/) is for audience research: where a defined audience gets information. [Exploding Topics](https://explodingtopics.com/) helps you notice topics that are gaining attention. [Google Trends](https://trends.google.com/) is the public view of search demand. None of the three is a replacement for BuzzSumo's web-content, PR, and influencer workflows. ContHunt is not a replacement for those workflows either. Native analytics is not a replacement for any of them, and none of them are a replacement for native analytics on an account you own.

Check each vendor's current coverage and plan limits before you buy. A starting-point table goes stale the moment a product drops a source or adds one. The job column goes stale much more slowly. Keep the job column when you refresh the prices.

## How much is BuzzSumo in 2026?

Searchers who ask how much BuzzSumo costs usually want two things. They want the number on the pricing page. They want a plain-language check of whether that number buys the job they have. This article can only do the second one with a clear conscience. The first one lives at [buzzsumo.com/pricing](https://buzzsumo.com/pricing/). Plans change. A figure copied into a post would be a liability the week the page changes.

The decision underneath the price is stable.

- Choose BuzzSumo when you need broad web, PR, influencer, and historical content research. The live plan has to include the sources and the exports you will use.
- Choose ContHunt when the core work is short-form video research across TikTok, Instagram Reels, and YouTube Shorts.
- Choose a hybrid when you need occasional web and PR research and you also have a daily creator-intelligence habit.

"Worth it" is not a property of the invoice. It is a property of the overlap between the invoice and the week. A solopreneur who publishes only short-form video should compare BuzzSumo with a narrower tool before paying for a broad database. A PR lead who lives in journalist outreach should not drop that database because a video tool is cheaper. Cheaper at the wrong job is a second tool you will still have to buy.

If the only output you can name is "we might look at trending articles sometime," you do not yet have a BuzzSumo workload. Write the weekly questions first. If those questions are about web articles, backlinks, and outreach, you are in the right conversation. If those questions are about hooks and on-screen text, you are in a different one.

## When is a broad content database the wrong bill?

A broad database is the wrong bill when the team's actual review is still manual and still about video. The failure mode is specific. The subscription covers articles, shares, and a PR graph. The Monday meeting still asks which opening line is showing up on Reels. It asks which caption structure keeps repeating on TikTok. It asks which public Shorts in the category are worth showing a creator. None of those questions were in the database you rented. Someone does the review by hand, in the apps, on a small sample, with no evidence attached.

That hand review is the workload ContHunt is for. ContHunt searches TikTok, Instagram Reels, and YouTube Shorts. Deep Search watches 500 to 1,000 videos in under five minutes and returns a ranked answer with the videos as evidence. You can argue with the answer because the videos are there. Tracking is experimental. It is not a PR wire and it does not include a journalist database.

There is a second way to reach the same research from an agent. The ContHunt MCP server is `https://mcp.conthunt.app`. An agent that calls it is still doing short-form research, not secretly querying BuzzSumo's web index. [Using ContHunt with AI agents through MCP](/resources/using-conthunt-with-ai-agents-through-mcp) describes that connection. [How Deep Search works](/resources/how-deep-search-works) describes the ranked answer the agent is asking for. Neither page turns ContHunt into a BuzzSumo replacement. If the prompt is "who are the journalists on this beat," you are in the wrong product. If the prompt is "which public hooks keep showing up for this product category," you are in the right one.

The risk to name in the budget meeting is not that BuzzSumo is expensive or that BuzzSumo is cheap. The risk is paying for a large content database while the hook, caption, and competitor-video review still happens somewhere else. That review is unpaid for and untracked. Fix the location of the work. Then the price has something to be compared with.

## How do you compare coverage without inventing a savings number?

Do not subtract a price you do not have from a price you do not have. A fake savings figure is worse than no figure, because finance will remember the savings and forget that you invented it. Compare jobs covered, and let the live prices enter only after both vendors' pages have been read.

An illustrative checklist, not a product score, uses six jobs:

1. Broad web content research.
2. PR or influencer workflows.
3. Search-demand validation.
4. Audience attention research.
5. Emerging-topic discovery.
6. Public short-form creative research.

On the starting-point table above, BuzzSumo is the better start for jobs 1 and 2. That is 2 of the 6 jobs on this illustrative list. Two divided by six is one third. A short-form team that buys only BuzzSumo has, on this list, covered one third of the jobs and has not covered job 6 at all.

A hybrid that keeps BuzzSumo for web and PR work and uses ContHunt for job 6 covers jobs 1, 2, and 6. That is 3 of 6, which is one half. The other half is still open: search demand, audience attention, and emerging topics. Three plus three is six. The hybrid did not "beat" BuzzSumo by a rating. It covered one additional job that BuzzSumo was not the starting point for. It left three jobs that belong to Google Trends, Search Console, SparkToro, or Exploding Topics.

If both products later have a real price in your note, you can divide that price by jobs you will actually run each week. Suppose, purely as arithmetic practice, that a plan displayed $100 a month. You will run 4 of these jobs on it every week, for 4 weeks. That is 16 job-runs, and 100 / 16 = 6.25 dollars per job-run. The $100 is not a BuzzSumo price and not a ContHunt price. It is a placeholder so the division can be checked. Replace it with the figure from the pricing page or the division is fiction. If you will run only 1 of the jobs, use the same pretend $100. Then 100 / 4 = 25 dollars per monthly run of that single job, across four weeks. The tool did not get worse. The overlap got thinner. That is the whole value test.

## What should a content-research budget checklist force you to admit?

Run this list before the card is charged. Each line is a reason to pause, not a reason to feel thorough.

- Have you written the sources you need: web, search, TikTok, Reels, Shorts, YouTube more broadly, or PR?
- Are you paying for historical data you will open, or for history that sounds reassuring?
- Do you need alerts, exports, and extra seats, or do you need a weekly research session?
- Have you checked native analytics before paying anyone for modeled data about your own account?
- Does the alternative lack a source you use every week, which would make the lower price a second purchase?
- Can you point to last month's questions and say which product would have answered each one?

Historical data is the line that inflates briefs. A five-year archive is worth paying for when you will compare this launch with the last three launches. It is not worth paying for when every question you asked last quarter was about the last two weeks of videos. Alerts have the same shape. An alert you will not staff is a notification you archive. Seats have the same shape. A fifth seat for someone who will not log in is not collaboration.

Native analytics comes before any modeled layer on your own channel. YouTube, Instagram, and TikTok already show you how your posts did, inside the limits of each product. A research tool that offers a smoother chart of your own account still needs to be checked against that native screen. Pay for native-quality reporting only when the export, the history, or the team workflow is the thing Insights or Studio will not give you.

## How does a hybrid stack stay honest over a quarter?

A hybrid stack uses BuzzSumo, or a similar web and PR database, for the weeks you have a web and PR question. It adds a short-form research workflow for the weeks you are briefing videos. It adds native analytics for the account you own. It is not three tools pointed at the same question.

Give each tool a day of the week or it will sprawl. An illustrative week, not a prescription: Monday, native analytics on what you published. Tuesday, short-form research on the category's public videos, with the videos kept as evidence. Wednesday, only if a web or PR question is actually on the board, the broad database. If Wednesday has no such question, you do not open the database to justify the seat. Three days, three jobs. A quarter is about 13 weeks. If the Wednesday question appears in 4 of those 13 weeks, the database was load-bearing on 4 / 13 of the weeks. Four divided by thirteen is not a price. It is a usage fact you can put next to the price once you have one. If the Tuesday question appears in 13 of 13 weeks, the short-form tool is the daily system and the database is the occasional one. Buying them in the reverse pattern, a daily database and an occasional video sample, is the mismatch this comparison is about.

Keep the notes in one place so the quarter can be audited. For each weekly question, write one line. Include the job number from the list of six, the tool you used, and whether the answer came back with evidence you can reopen. A line that says "BuzzSumo, felt useful" is not an audit. A line that says "job 6, public Shorts, 12 videos saved with the hook tagged" can be checked. Twelve is an illustrative count of saved examples, not a required sample size. The point of the line is that a person can open the 12.

Renewal is the moment to repeat the field worksheet. Open the pricing page again. If the plan name, the sources, or the export limit changed, the old comparison is void. Write a new one. Do not renew on the memory of a decision that was about a different plan.

The comparison ends in a sentence you can say without a chart. Use BuzzSumo when broad web and PR research is the work you will do often enough to justify whatever the pricing page says today. Use ContHunt when the daily job is finding and analyzing public short-form examples. Use both when both jobs are real. Use native analytics to judge your own posts either way. Leave every other dollar figure on the vendor's page, where it can be updated by the vendor instead of by a blog.
