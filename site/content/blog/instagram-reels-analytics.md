Review an Instagram Reel by reading watch time next to reach, saves, and shares, then compare Reels that share a topic and a length over the same amount of time since posting. Your recent posts are the baseline. A save rate, a share count, or a single view spike does not establish that a Reel will travel further, and the Meta announcements cited here do not publish a retention percentage that unlocks distribution. Write down the label Instagram shows you. If a field is missing, leave it missing. Public likes on someone else's Reel are not a substitute for your watch time.

## What should Instagram Reels analytics decide?

Instagram Reels analytics should decide the next edit, not crown a winner. The decision is small on purpose: keep the opening, move the payoff earlier, or stop repeating a format that people do not save or send. A dashboard that cannot support one of those sentences is a screenshot.

Two boundaries keep the review honest. First, metric names are not interchangeable. Views, plays, reach, and watch time answer different questions, and Instagram has changed how some of those words are defined. Second, another creator's private insights are not available. You can watch their public Reel and read public counts. You cannot import their retention curve. ContHunt sits on the public side of that line. It searches TikTok, Instagram Reels, and YouTube Shorts so you can compare openings. It does not display a competitor's Instagram Insights.

Use your own account as the only place where watch time is a measurement. Use other people's Reels as creative evidence. Mixing the two is how a borrowed view count becomes a fake benchmark.

## What did Meta publish about watch time?

On April 14, 2023, Meta's newsroom post [New Features on Instagram Reels: Trends, Editing and Gifts](https://about.fb.com/news/2023/04/instagram-reels-trending-audio-and-gifts-updates/) said Instagram was adding two Reels insights: total watch time and average watch time. The definitions in that post are:

- Total watch time is the total time the Reel was played, including time spent replaying it.
- Average watch time is the average time spent playing the Reel, calculated by dividing watch time by the number of total plays.

Meta's own illustration of the second definition: if average watch time is 17 seconds, then among people who watched the Reel, the average time played was 17 seconds. That sentence explains the unit. It is not a pass mark, and it is not a length you should edit toward. A 17-second average on a much shorter Reel and a 17-second average on a much longer Reel are different outcomes. Always write the Reel's length next to the average.

The same announcement said the point of watch time is to see where people stayed and where a stronger hook might hold them, and that a creator might reshoot a section that ran too long. It also said creators would get a notification about new followers from Reels. Those are product descriptions from April 2023. They explain what the metrics were built to show. They do not prove that your current app build uses the same screen, the same words, or the same notification.

## What did the November 2023 update add?

On November 15, 2023, Meta's post [New Ways to Create Content on Instagram](https://about.fb.com/news/2023/11/new-ways-to-create-content-on-instagram/) said Instagram had introduced a Reels metric called Replays and had updated the definition of Reels Plays to include Replays as well as Initial Plays. The post says you may see Reels Plays rise because of that change. A jump in plays around a definition change is not, by itself, evidence that the Reel improved. If you compare a Reel from before the change with a Reel from after it, write down which definition you are using. If you do not know, do not compare the play counts as if they were the same unit.

The same post said that, over the following months, creators would be able to see how many people were watching a Reel moment by moment on an interactive retention chart. "Over the coming months" is not "every account has this chart today." If your insights show a retention chart, use it. If they do not, do not backfill one from likes. Mark the field unavailable.

Dated announcements are origins, not a current interface contract. When you record a number, record the date you saw it and the label on the screen.

## Which question does each metric answer?

Give every field one question. If you cannot name the question, you are collecting the number out of habit.

| What you record | The question it can answer | The comparison that misleads |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Total watch time and average watch time, beside length | Did the opening lead into a payoff people stayed for, including replays in the total? | Judging a short loop and a long tutorial by seconds alone |
| Plays or views, and reach, under the labels you see | Is this broad exposure, repeat playing, or both? | Treating plays and reach as the same count, especially after Replays were added to Plays |
| Saves | Did someone want to come back to the information? | Calling every save a future reach event |
| Shares | Was there a reason to send this to a specific person? | Assuming a share applies a known boost |
| Follows from the Reel, if that label is shown | Did this topic give a reason to follow? | Assigning every new follower on the account to one Reel |
| A retention chart, if you have one | Where did watching drop or replay? | Drawing a curve you were not shown |

None of these rows is a ranking of Instagram's internal weights. They are questions for a review. A high save count with weak watch time can mean a small group found a list useful, or it can mean very little, depending on the denominator. Write the denominator down.

If you compute a rate, name it. Saves divided by accounts reached and saves divided by plays are different rates. This illustrative pair uses round figures so the arithmetic is visible. It is not an account export.

| Illustrative Reel | Plays | Accounts reached | Saves | Saves per play | Saves per account reached |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Form check | 1,000 | 800 | 40 | 4% | 5% |
| Myth bust | 1,000 | 1,000 | 20 | 2% | 2% |
| Gear list | 1,000 | 600 | 30 | 3% | 5% |

Form check: 40 divided by 1,000 plays is 4%, and 40 divided by 800 reached is 5%. Myth bust: 20 divided by 1,000 is 2% on both rates because plays and reach match in the example. Gear list: 30 divided by 1,000 is 3%, and 30 divided by 600 is 5%. Form check and the gear list tie on saves per account reached and do not tie on saves per play. A review that quotes "5%" without the denominator has mixed two different claims. Leave a rate blank when the denominator is missing or zero.

Average watch time has the same discipline. Meta's definition divides watch time by total plays. An illustrative check: 12,000 seconds of total watch time and 1,000 plays is 12 seconds average. 8,000 seconds and 1,000 plays is 8 seconds. 15,000 seconds and 1,000 plays is 15 seconds. Those three averages are comparable with each other only if the Reels are a similar length and the plays were counted the same way. They are not a target, and 15 seconds is not "better" than 12 if the longer average belongs to a Reel that never reaches its point.

## How do you build a comparison worksheet?

Pick a handful of recent Reels with the same job. Three tutorials, or three entertainment beats. Do not put a meme and a product demo in one ranking because both were posted on a Tuesday.

For each Reel, record:

- The Reel URL, the publish date, the topic, and the length.
- The opening promise, in a few words, and when the payoff actually appears.
- The date you are looking, and how long after publishing that is. Compare day-2 with day-2, not day-2 with day-14.
- The watch-time fields you can see, the play or view label, reach if it is shown, saves, shares, and follows attributed to the Reel if that label exists.
- Anything else that breaks the comparison: a collaboration, a paid boost, a remix, or a Reel you posted and then edited.
- One change you will test next. One.

Read promotion before you read talent. A boosted Reel and an organic Reel do not belong in the same "what worked" sentence unless the boost is the thing you are studying. A collaboration brings an audience you may not have again. Note it, and do not let it set the baseline for a Reel you will post alone.

Keep the sheet stable. Same columns, same rate definitions, same age window. A new column is fine when Instagram shows you a new label. Renaming plays to "views" because that sounds cleaner is not fine. The November 2023 note about Plays including Replays is exactly why the label matters.

[Reels versus Shorts analytics](/blog/instagram-reels-vs-youtube-shorts-analytics) covers the trap of moving a number from one app into the other. A Shorts view and a Reel play are not one metric with two logos. If you publish in both places, keep two sheets.

## How do you turn one finding into one edit?

Suppose a tutorial draws comments that repeat the question, and the average watch time is short relative to your other tutorials of a similar length. A reasonable editorial test is to show the finished result in the first frames and shorten the setup. Write that hypothesis before you post the next version, so you cannot retrofit the story after you see the numbers.

Judge the next version against similar tutorials at the same age. A better watch time is a reason to try that shape again. It does not isolate the edit as the cause. The topic's demand, the mix of people who saw it, and whatever else you changed will move too. If you changed the hook, the length, and the cover frame, you learned that the bundle was different, not which piece mattered.

A finding that should not become an edit: "saves were high, so the next ten Reels should beg for saves." Saves are evidence that someone wanted the information later. Asking for an empty comment, or for a save that the Reel did not earn, does not reproduce the metric. Make the thing worth keeping, then measure.

## Why can't public research replace Insights?

Public research answers a different question: what are other Reels in this topic showing in the first seconds, and what are people replying in public? That is a briefing input. It is not a watch-time report. A public like count does not reveal average watch time, replays, or a retention shape. Anyone who offers those numbers for a competitor's Reel is estimating, and the estimate should be labeled as an estimate or thrown out.

A tool that only shows public counts should not be described as analytics for an account you do not own.

## What belongs on the review checklist?

Run this before you change the content plan:

- The Reels share a topic and a similar length.
- The observation window is the same age for each Reel.
- Labels match the screen, and any rate has a written denominator.
- Plays from before and after a definition change are not treated as one series unless you noted the change.
- Missing private fields were left blank.
- Boosts, collaborations, and remixes are marked.
- The 17-second figure, if you mention it, is identified as Meta's April 2023 illustration of average watch time, not as your goal.
- Exactly one edit is queued for the next test.

Instagram Reels analytics is a comparison you can repeat: watch time, reach, saves, and shares, on similar Reels, at the same age, with your account as the baseline. The April 2023 definitions and the November 2023 Plays and retention notes tell you what those words were built to mean. They do not set a score that makes a Reel spread.
