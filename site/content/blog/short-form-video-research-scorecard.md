A short-form video research scorecard turns a collection of TikTok, Instagram Reels and YouTube Shorts links into a brief someone can check. Copy the table below, keep the source and observation date beside each row, and describe what the viewer can actually see. This is a research template, not a benchmark of all videos or a prediction of views.

## Copy the scorecard

Use one row per public video. Capture a fixed observation time: public counts can change between visits. Leave a field blank when the platform does not show it; blank is not zero.

| Field | Record |
| --- | --- |
| Research question | One decision, such as which product demonstrations to test |
| Source | Platform, original public video URL and creator handle |
| Dates | Publish date if visible, plus the time and time zone when you observed it |
| Topic and audience | The product or problem shown, and your interpretation of who it addresses |
| Opening | What appears or is said first; quote only what you can verify |
| Demonstration and proof | The action shown and the evidence presented in the video |
| Visible outcome | Views, likes and comments only where displayed; keep each label and timestamp |
| Next test | One original idea to test, with the evidence link beside it |
| Limits | Missing counts, uncertain relevance, and any creator claims you could not independently verify |

For a spreadsheet, copy this header row. Add the entries yourself; the column names are not measured data.

```csv
research_question,platform,video_url,creator,published_at,observed_at,topic,audience_inference,opening,demonstration,proof,views,likes,comments,limitations,next_test
```

## How do you select examples?

1. Define the audience, topic, platforms and date window before searching. Write down the exact search phrases and when you ran them.
2. Save the original public links and keep a record of duplicates and exclusions. Check that the video actually addresses the question; a keyword in a caption is not enough on its own.
3. Compare videos of similar age, format and topic where possible. Record account size when it is visible and useful, but do not invent a baseline when it is missing.
4. Keep contrasting examples as well as strong-looking ones. A shortlist of successful videos cannot tell you how often an approach succeeds across all uploads.
5. Reopen the evidence before sharing the brief. Label observations, creator claims and your own inferences separately.

For an illustrative comparison, a skincare team might select public videos about one specific moisturizer question across all three platforms. The exact search phrases and selected dates belong in the sheet. If one platform returns fewer relevant examples, report the shortfall instead of filling the rows with unrelated videos.

## How do you score a video consistently?

A useful score names the question and the evidence. For a creator brief, rate each of these four checks as **present**, **absent**, or **unclear**: the opening names the topic; the video demonstrates the product or method; a visible example supports the claim; and the payoff matches the opening. Define what counts before reviewing the set, and link to each video so a second reviewer can disagree.

Do not turn those four checks into a claim that a video caused sales or kept viewers watching. An opening can be clear while its performance is unknown. When reviewers disagree, preserve the disagreement and inspect the video again rather than silently changing the label.

## What can public metrics tell you?

Views and visible engagement describe a post at an observed time. They are not another account's retention curve, conversion rate or revenue. A like-to-view ratio, if you calculate one, is likes divided by views at the same observation time, multiplied by 100; it is not a universal engagement-rate definition. Record whether counts are missing, and never divide by zero.

If you have two timestamped observations of the same metric, the difference is the later count minus the earlier count. It does not reveal when the change happened or why. For your own accounts, use the platform's first-party analytics to evaluate watch behavior and conversion where available.

## Turn the evidence into a brief

Write the audience, the problem, two or three observed patterns with links, and one original test for each pattern. Include counterexamples and the limits of the sample. Then state the decision: what to film, what to hold constant, and how the team will review its own results after publishing.
