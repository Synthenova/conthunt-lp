A content idea is a specific promise you can produce and then judge, not a topic label such as "productivity" or "skincare." A content idea that is ready to make names the person it is for, the outcome it promises, and the proof you can show. You find those ideas by narrowing a subject until a stranger knows who the post is for, by reading public short-form video for patterns that already repeat, and by dropping anything you cannot film with proof you have. ContHunt searches TikTok, Instagram Reels, and YouTube Shorts, so the narrowing can start from public videos instead of a blank page. The patterns below are templates to adapt. None of them guarantees reach.

## What separates a content idea from a topic

A topic is a subject. A content idea is a decision about what the viewer gets in this post. "Email" is a topic. "The three lines I delete from a cold email before I send it, shown on a real draft" is a content idea. The second one can be filmed this week and argued with. The first one can fill a calendar without teaching anyone anything.

Use four checks. A content idea is ready when you can say yes to each line.

| Check | Pass | Fail |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Person | A named situation, not "everyone" | "People who want to grow" |
| Promise | One sentence a viewer would recognize in the first line | A theme with no outcome |
| Proof | A screen, object, number you measured, or story you lived | A claim you would have to invent |
| Pattern | You have seen the structure in more than one public video, or you are deliberately testing a gap | One viral clip treated as a law |

In an illustrative scoring pass, "productivity tips" fails all four checks: no person, no promise, no proof, no pattern. That is 0 of 4. "A two-minute kitchen reset for parents after the kids are in bed, filmed on tonight's actual counter" names the person, the promise, the proof, and can be checked against other public cleanup videos. That is 4 of 4. Zero plus four is the whole comparison. The count is a filter for your list, not a score a platform assigned.

## How to narrow a topic until you can film it

Start broad, then add a person and a situation. Stop when the idea would look wrong if you handed it to a different audience.

An illustrative chain for productivity:

1. Productivity.
2. Deep work.
3. Deep work for parents of young children.
4. A 20-minute reset after bedtime, before you open a laptop.

Step 4 is a content idea. Step 1 is a bin. The same chain works in any field. "Insurance" becomes "the one question to ask before you renew a renter's policy after you buy a bike." Specificity is how a stranger recognizes the post. It is not a claim that a narrower topic receives a known lift. Nobody outside your account can promise that.

If you cannot name the person, you are not ready to write a hook. If you cannot name the proof, you are not ready to film. Save the topic in a parking list and move on.

## Where a week of content ideas should come from

Content ideas come from four places. Use all four. A week built from only one of them goes stale or turns into a diary nobody asked for.

### Public videos in a narrow slice

Search the specific phrase, not the giant category. On TikTok, Instagram Reels, and YouTube Shorts, look for a structure that appears more than once: the same kind of opening, the same kind of proof, the same missing step. Write the pattern in one sentence before you save a dozen links. "They show the price on screen in the first second" is a note. "This one did well" is not.

When the niche is too large to scroll, Deep Search watches 500 to 1,000 videos in under five minutes and returns a ranked answer with the videos as evidence. Use that when you need a sample, and keep the videos attached to the claim. A pattern you cannot point at is a hunch. One spike is not a category. [Instagram Reels content ideas](/blog/instagram-reels-content-ideas) and [YouTube Shorts content ideas](/blog/youtube-shorts-content-ideas) apply the same filter on a single platform.

### Questions people still ask

Read the comments under the strongest public videos in the set. A repeated question is a content idea the current winners did not finish. Write it as "they show X and never show Y." Your version should add Y, not re-edit their footage.

### Proof only you have

Your client work, your mistakes, your screen, and your process are the ideas a stranger cannot scrape. A tool can show you which structures are common. It cannot supply a story you did not live. If the proof is confidential, change the identifying details or skip the idea.

### A structure borrowed from another field

A format that is ordinary in one field can be fresh in yours. An illustrative transfer: "what I would not buy" audits are common in software reviews and less common in explainers about home insurance. The structure travels. The footage should still be yours. This is a writing method, not a button inside a research tool.

## How to cut a long list before you produce anything

Generate freely, then delete in one sitting. Production time is the scarce part.

1. Write each content idea as a sentence the viewer could repeat after one second of the video.
2. Name the person and the situation on the same line.
3. Name the proof you can show without a new purchase or a new permission.
4. Mark whether you have seen that structure more than once in public videos this month.
5. Cut every idea you cannot produce with the tools and hours you already have.
6. Keep a short list. A parking list is allowed. A calendar full of maybes is not.

Do not treat a quiet topic as a moral failure, and do not treat a loud topic as an assignment. A topic with no proof you can show is a bad content idea even if the comments are busy. A topic you have seen only once may be an outlier. Note it, and wait for a second example before you build a series.

Leave a publishing day empty rather than fill it with an idea that failed the checks. A cadence you can sustain at that standard beats a daily slot of generic topics. Three finished videos in a week is one sustainable pace for a small team. It is an illustrative planning choice, not a measured rule about how often to post.

## Which content idea patterns are worth adapting

The patterns below are starting points from public formats creators already use. Swap in your person, promise, and proof. Skip any pattern whose proof you do not have.

### Teach a fix

Teaching patterns work when the viewer can copy a move.

- The three-second fix shows one common mistake and the correction immediately. The mistake has to be visible, not implied.
- The myth correction names a belief your niche repeats and shows why it fails on a real example. Cite what you checked. Do not invent a study.
- The tool stack shows the few tools you actually use for one outcome. A drawer of logos is not a stack.
- The glossary defines one term your buyer hears and does not understand. One term per post. A long list of definitions is a different piece.
- The time-lapse shows a long process in a short video, with the decision points left in. Pretty speed is not the idea. The decisions are.

### Show the work

Behind-the-scenes patterns work when the dull part is the useful part.

- A day-in-the-life that includes the waiting, the admin, and the redos is more useful than a highlight reel.
- A mistake reflection needs the decision that caused the mistake and the check you use now. A confession without a lesson is not a content idea.
- A client question, with permission or with the details removed, is already phrased the way buyers talk.
- A workspace or screen tour works if you explain one choice, such as why a reference sits next to the keyboard. A pan across a room is not an idea.
- Decision logic explains why you picked one strategy and rejected another. Show the rejected option.

### Ask the viewer to do something small

Participation patterns work when the ask is easy to answer.

- A vote on the next topic needs two real options you are willing to make.
- A reaction to a public news item needs your own added step. Reading a headline aloud is not enough.
- A short challenge should be something the viewer can do without buying your product.
- A fill-in-the-blank prompt gives the comments a shape. "Thoughts?" does not.
- A live audit of a follower's public page needs a checklist you apply the same way every time, and it needs kindness. Do not mock a stranger for reach.

### Borrow a proven shape without borrowing a fake statistic

These shapes are common because they make a promise fast. The danger is the number in the first line.

- A data reveal only works when you did the review. State the sample you actually have.
- A crossover names two fields and the specific practice that moved from one to the other.
- An expert critique reacts to a public example in your trade and adds a correction.
- A silent tutorial carries every step on screen, with text or a clear visual order. The idea is the order, not the absence of a voice.
- A tool comparison uses a task you ran on both tools. Do not publish a speed or price claim you did not measure.
- A zero-to-one story is a sequence you completed. Skip it if you are guessing the middle.
- A forecast is labeled as a forecast. Separate what you saw in public videos from what you expect next.
- An information-gap post names the step the popular videos skipped and then teaches that step.

If you want a caption that reports a review, fill it with the real scope. Do not publish a round number you did not count.

```text
I reviewed the public videos we saved for [niche] this week. The opening that showed up most often was [pattern]. The step those videos skipped was [gap].
```

Deep Search can support the first sentence only when the result tells you how many videos it watched and you link or save that evidence. "I analyzed 1,000 videos" is not a template to paste. A false count is worse than no hook. The [3-second rule](/resources/the-3-second-rule-a-hook-taxonomy) is about how the opening is built, not a license to invent a result.

### Posts that are not a finished video

Some content ideas are lighter than a produced short and still specific.

- An A/B carousel shows two real versions and asks which one solves the problem. The two versions have to differ in one way.
- A poll offers two choices you will actually use.
- A knowledge-gap post states a common belief, then the observation that complicates it, and invites a correction.
- A screenshot or screen recording shows the workflow, including the unglamorous menu.
- A voice memo over a still image fits when the value is the explanation and the visual would be fake polish.
- A community spotlight needs the other person's permission and a concrete result.
- A crowd-sourced note asks for one tip and later publishes the ones you checked.
- An AMA works when you state the kind of question you will answer. An open "ask me anything" becomes a pile.

## How to use a writing model without handing it the strategy

A writing model is a draft tool. It is not the source of the content idea. The order that holds up is simple.

1. You choose the pattern from videos and notes you can point to.
2. You write the person, the promise, and the proof in your own words.
3. A model drafts a few openings and a rough order of shots.
4. You delete claims you cannot support, lines that do not sound like you, and any count the model invented.
5. You add one detail only you could know, and you write the comment or caption you are willing to stand behind.

Paste the pattern and the constraints. Do not paste private analytics, customer names, or a request for a fake study. Generative video and synthetic voice are optional production tools with the same rule: if you did not do the thing on screen, do not imply that you did. AI output still needs a person who is accountable for the facts.

## How to split a week between teaching, story, and a pitch

This guide uses a planning split of 70 percent teaching, 20 percent story, and 10 percent direct pitch. That split is a planning rule for keeping a feed from becoming a catalog. It is not a measured law of any algorithm.

On an illustrative slate of 10 posts, 70 percent is 7 teaching posts, 20 percent is 2 story posts, and 10 percent is 1 pitch. Seven plus two plus one is 10. The pitch still has to pass the four checks. A discount with no person and no proof is not the 10 percent. It is a failed idea that happens to mention a product.

An illustrative week for a freelance bookkeeper:

- Teaching: a reconciliation mistake, a glossary of "accrual," a screen recording of a monthly close, a myth about receipt photos, a three-tool stack, a time-lapse of a messy folder becoming a folder with names, and a client question about sales tax.
- Story: a mistake that cost a rework afternoon, and why the desk is set up a certain way.
- Pitch: one post that offers a paid cleanup, with the scope and who it is for.

Swap the trade. Keep the ratio only if it matches the work you want to be known for. A founder in a launch week may run a different mix on purpose. Write the mix down so a busy week does not become ten pitches.

## What to check before a content idea goes on the calendar

Read this list on the idea, not on the topic.

- The person and the situation fit in one line.
- The promise is visible in the opening, not only in the caption.
- The proof is already in hand.
- You have either a repeated public pattern or a deliberate gap, and you know which.
- A model did not add a statistic, a price, or a quote you cannot source.
- You know what you will look at in your own analytics after it is published. Watch time and retention for your account live in the platform's native analytics, not in a third-party guess about someone else's video.
- The idea is teaching, story, or pitch, and you know which one it is.

If a line fails, the content idea goes back to the parking list. The calendar should contain posts you could brief to someone else without a meeting.
