Metricool pricing is the cost of scheduling and connected-account reporting, not a number a blog can freeze. Teams open Metricool to plan posts, connect more than one brand, and report on accounts they are allowed to access. It is the wrong comparison for private watch time, and the wrong comparison for research on public TikToks, Reels, and Shorts you do not manage. Read the live plan page on the day you buy. Write down brands, seats, history, exports, and which networks are included. Price only alternatives that do the same job. A static table of tiers and scores is not a quote. This guide is that comparison, including where Hootsuite, Buffer, Sprout Social, and ContHunt belong.

## What job Metricool pricing is paying for

Metricool pricing pays for operations: a calendar, connected profiles, and reports on those profiles. Judge it on that job.

People keep Metricool on the shortlist because the product is built for small teams and agencies that post often and do not want a heavyweight suite for a simple calendar. The buying question is whether the plan you would actually use includes the brands, the people, and the networks you already have. A cheaper plan that omits the network you post on is not cheaper. It is a second purchase waiting to happen.

Metricool does not become a short-form research tool because a plan includes analytics. Connected analytics describe accounts you authorized. They do not describe a stranger's private retention, and they do not watch a niche of public videos and return a cited pattern. If your week is "queue the posts, then export a client report," you are in Metricool's job. If your week is "which hooks are showing up on public Reels before we brief a creator," you are in a different job, described below, and a lower subscription will not do it.

Write the job in one sentence before you open a pricing page. An illustrative sentence, not a vendor slogan: "Three people schedule five brands on Instagram and TikTok and export a monthly report." Every later line on the plan page is a yes or no against that sentence.

## What to copy off the live pricing page

Copy facts from Metricool's own pricing page into a one-page note. Do not copy them from a roundup, including this one.

Use the same rows for every tool so the comparison is a table you control:

| Row | What you write down | Why it changes the price |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Billing unit | Monthly, annual, or both, and whether annual is prepaid | A prepaid year is not a monthly price if you might leave |
| Brands or profiles | How many are included, and what an extra one costs | Agencies run out of brand slots before they run out of features |
| Seats | How many people can log in, and whether roles differ | A plan that fits the brands and not the team is a second plan |
| Networks | Which platforms, and which profile types | A missing network is a different product, not a discount |
| History and exports | How far back reports go, and which fields export | A dashboard you cannot hand to a client is not a report |
| Limits | Posts, scheduled items, or similar caps, in the vendor's words | A cap under your real volume is an overage, not a feature |
| Add-ons | Anything listed apart from the plan price | The comparable price is plan plus required add-ons |

If a row is missing, write "not stated." Do not fill the gap from memory or from an old article. Plan names change. Included counts change. A feature shown on a marketing page is not automatically in the tier you clicked. The same caution is the core of [social media analytics tools](/blog/social-media-analytics-tools): confirm what a connected report actually imports before you compare numbers across products.

Screenshot the page with the date in the filename. When someone forwards a price from a chat thread, you can answer with the screenshot instead of a debate.

Compare Metricool with a tool that does the same job, or admit you are buying a second job.

Hootsuite is the workflow comparison when the team is large enough that roles, approvals, and many networks are the actual requirement. It is not "the expensive Metricool." It is a different scope. Read [Hootsuite alternatives for startups](/blog/hootsuite-alternatives-startups) if the company is still small and a suite would sit unused. Buffer is the comparison when people bounce off large dashboards and the work really is a calendar. Sprout Social is the comparison when the buying group is already looking at a full social suite. [Sprout Social alternatives](/blog/sprout-social-alternatives) is the page for that fork. None of these sentences include a price, because a price that is not on the vendor's page today is not a fact.

ContHunt is the comparison when the sentence you wrote is about public short-form video, not about the queue. Putting ContHunt in a "cheapest scheduler" column is a category error. It does not become a scheduler because you wanted one product to do both jobs.

## What ContHunt is, if the job is public short-form research

ContHunt searches TikTok, Instagram Reels, and YouTube Shorts. It is a research tool for public videos, not a Metricool plan with a different logo.

Deep Search watches 500 to 1,000 videos in under five minutes and returns a ranked answer with the videos as evidence. You use it to answer questions a calendar cannot answer: which openings keep appearing for a product, which formats a niche is repeating, which claims show up on the competitors' public posts. The rank is a judgment of fit to the question you asked. It is not measured viewer retention. Retention of that kind stays in the native dashboard of the account that posted the video.

Tracking in ContHunt is experimental. It is for following public accounts and posts over time. It is not a connected-account report, and it is not a white-label client deck. If the contract you sell is "we schedule and we export your own numbers," Metricool's job is still the one to price. If the contract you sell is "we show you what public videos in the category are doing," price ContHunt as that second job, or as the only job, and do not force it through a brand-slot calculator built for schedulers.

## How to do the cost arithmetic without inventing a quote

Cost per brand is a division you perform on numbers you copied today. It is not a score this page can publish for Metricool.

The formula is: comparable monthly cost divided by brands you will actually use. Use brands you will use, not brands the plan allows and you will leave empty. Empty slots make a plan look cheap per brand and expensive in practice.

This worked example is illustrative. The units are not dollars, and they are not a Metricool, Hootsuite, Buffer, Sprout Social, or ContHunt price.

- A plan is listed at 90 units per month and includes 6 brands. You will use all 6. Cost per brand in use is 90 divided by 6, which is 15 units.
- The same 90-unit plan includes 6 brands, but you will use 3. Cost per brand in use is 90 divided by 3, which is 30 units. The plan did not get more expensive. The unused slots stopped subsidizing the arithmetic.
- A second tool for a different job is 30 units per month, and it serves the same 3 brands. Combined cost per brand in use is (90 plus 30) divided by 3, which is 120 divided by 3, which is 40 units. You may still want both, because the jobs differ. You should not describe the 30-unit tool as "more expensive Metricool."

Annual prepay needs a second line. Illustrative again: 480 units billed once for 12 months is 480 divided by 12, which is 40 units in a monthly-equivalent column, but only if you keep the plan for the year. If you leave after 3 months and the vendor does not refund, the cost of those months is 480 divided by 3, which is 160 units each, not 40. Put both numbers in the note. Teams that compare only the monthly equivalent systematically favor prepaid plans they will not finish.

Add required add-ons before you divide. If the export you need is a 20-unit add-on, the 90-unit plan costs 110 units for the comparison. Illustrative check: 110 divided by 6 brands in use is 18.33 units per brand, not 15. Rounding to 15 would hide the add-on. Keep one decimal or keep the fraction. Do not round a buying note into a slogan.

Never divide a research tool's price by Metricool's brand count and call the result efficiency. Brand slots are a scheduler constraint. A research question does not consume one slot per brand in that same sense. Compare like with like, then decide whether you need two invoices.

## What a second tool should not duplicate

A second subscription should remove a gap, not redraw charts you already have.

Metricool, or whichever scheduler you pick, should remain the place that holds the queue and the connected-account export. Native dashboards remain the place that defines views, reach, and watch time for accounts you manage. Instagram, TikTok, and YouTube each define those words. A scheduler's report can import, rename, or omit a field. When a client asks for the number that matches the app, open the app. [Instagram Reels vs YouTube Shorts analytics](/blog/instagram-reels-vs-youtube-shorts-analytics) shows how far those native definitions already diverge. A third-party chart that blends them will not repair the definitions.

Public research should stay in its own column. It answers "what are other people posting," using videos anyone can watch. It does not answer "what was our retention at second four." Buying ContHunt to avoid opening YouTube Studio will disappoint you. Buying Metricool to avoid watching competitor videos will also disappoint you. The expensive mistake is a suite that claims both and does the one you needed thinly.

Listening and mention-tracking, if you need them, are a third job again. Do not assume they are inside a scheduling tier because the word "analytics" appears on the page. Confirm the query limits and the networks in writing, on the plan you will pay for.

## A checklist before you pick a plan

Walk the checklist on a call, with the pricing page open. If the vendor cannot show the row, the row is a no.

1. Read your one-sentence job out loud. If the sentence is about a calendar, stay in the scheduler column. If it is about public videos, leave that column.
2. Count brands you will connect in the next quarter, not brands you hope to win.
3. Count people who need a login. Check whether the plan's seats match that count.
4. List the networks. Strike any plan that does not include them.
5. Export one report during the trial, using the date range you actually send to a client.
6. Write the comparable monthly cost as plan plus required add-ons. Apply the prepaid warning if you might cancel inside the year.
7. Divide by brands in use only after that cost is complete. Show the arithmetic in the note.
8. If you add a second product, label its job in the same sentence as its price. Do not merge the invoices and then declare a winner.

A plan that fails steps 4 or 5 is not a bargain. A plan that passes them and costs more than a thinner plan can still be the right buy, because the thinner plan failed a row you need.
