TikTok analytics is the account-owner view of how your videos reach people and whether those people stay. Written by the ContHunt team. The metrics that should change the next upload are completion, rewatches, shares, and whether the viewers look like the audience you meant to earn. A raw view count does not show that the hook worked. A like count is a weaker signal than a full watch or a send. Review the last 7 days, name the topic, opening line, and edit pattern behind the strongest signal for the effort, and repeat that structure. Ignore a view spike from the wrong people. TikTok analytics improves search visibility only indirectly, when a video people finish is easier for the platform to classify. Private TikTok analytics stay inside TikTok. Public video research is a separate job.

## What is TikTok analytics?

TikTok analytics is the set of measurements TikTok shows to the person who manages the account. It describes reach, attention, and audience for videos that account posted. It is not the public view counter a stranger can see on a profile. It is not a cross-platform research file of other creators' videos.

That distinction decides which tool you open. Owner analytics can speak to completion and rewatches because the platform recorded the watch. A public profile usually cannot. ContHunt searches public videos on TikTok, Instagram Reels, and YouTube Shorts. [How Deep Search works](/resources/how-deep-search-works). It watches 500 to 1,000 of those public videos in under five minutes and returns a ranked answer with the videos as evidence. ContHunt does not log into your TikTok account and read your private retention curve. Tracking in ContHunt is experimental, and even then it follows public signals, not the analytics screen inside TikTok.

Use TikTok analytics to judge your own uploads. Use public research when the question is what other people posted, how those videos open, and which patterns repeat. Mixing the two produces false confidence. A public view count is not your completion rate, and your completion rate is not a map of the category.

## Which TikTok metrics should you read first?

Read TikTok analytics in an order that matches how a video earns more distribution. Attention comes before applause.

Completion is the first check. Completion means people stayed through the video, or through whatever "watched full video" style figure your analytics screen actually shows. If the opening fails, the rest of the edit rarely gets a fair test. The label in the product can change. The question does not: did viewers reach the end, or did they leave while the hook was still talking?

Rewatches are the second check. A rewatch means someone played the video again. That is a stronger hint of interest than a like, because it costs time. Use rewatches beside completion. A video people finish once and never replay can still be a clear explainer. A video people replay can be dense, funny, or unfinished in a useful way. Either pattern is information. Neither pattern is visible in the view total alone.

Shares are the third check. A share means someone sent the video onward. The source of a view can be the recommendation system. The source of a share is a person deciding another person should see it. Likes are worth a glance and a weak conclusion. Likes do not tell you the video held attention. Comments matter when they show the right people understood the topic. A comment that argues with the point is still evidence of comprehension. A pile of generic praise is weaker.

Follower quality sits over those counts. A video can collect views from people who will never follow, save, comment in a useful way, or buy. Audience quality is whether the people who watched are the people you meant. Follower count growth, on its own, does not prove quality if the new followers do not match the account's subject. A flat follower line does not prove failure. The right people may have saved or shared a video that was never designed to convert to a follow.

| Read this | The question it answers | Do not conclude |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Completion | Did people stay through the video? | That a high view count means the hook worked |
| Rewatches | Did people spend extra time on it? | That replays equal revenue |
| Shares | Did a person pass it on? | That likes are the same kind of effort |
| Audience fit | Were the viewers the ones you wanted? | That any spike is a good spike |
| Likes | Did some viewers tap a low-effort response? | That attention or distribution is explained |

TikTok's screens group these ideas under overview, per-video, and audience views, and the menu names move. Follow the questions. Do not wait for a perfect export before you decide what to film next.

## Why is a view count a weak TikTok metric?

A view count answers how many times a video was counted as seen under TikTok's current definition of a view. It does not answer who stayed, who left, or who was the wrong crowd. Platforms do not even count a "view" the same way as each other. For that cross-platform limit, [Reading virality beyond views](/resources/reading-virality-beyond-views) is the companion explanation. Inside one TikTok account, the problem is simpler and still serious: the biggest number on the screen is often the least decisive one.

A video can travel to people who do not care about the topic. They generate views, a few likes, and no follows, saves, or useful comments. The real test is whether watchers are likely to follow, save, comment, or buy. That is audience quality. A vanity spike fails the test when the comments are off-topic or the profile does not gain people who match the subject.

Traffic source, when the analytics screen shows it, is how you catch that mismatch. Views from people who already follow you are a different event from views the recommendation system tried on strangers. One is loyalty. The other is a test of the hook and the topic label. Compare them before you call a video a hit. If your screen does not break out a source you hoped to see, do not invent the split. Use the attention metrics you do have.

Do not borrow another account's view total as a target. You cannot see that account's completion. A public number and your private number are not a pair.

## How do you compare your own videos without a fake benchmark?

TikTok does not need an outside "good completion rate" for the weekly decision. You need a comparison against your own recent uploads. The figures in this section are illustrative. They are not a TikTok export, not a benchmark, and not a standard you should hit. They exist so the arithmetic is checkable. If your analytics screen shows a completion percentage or an average watch time, use that field. Rank your posts against each other, instead of requiring a raw full-watch count.

Illustrative posts from one account, over the last 7 days:

| Post | Views | Full watches | Shares | Follows you noted with the post |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | 8,000 | 3,200 | 160 | 40 |
| B | 20,000 | 4,000 | 100 | 15 |
| C | 5,000 | 2,500 | 200 | 60. |

Completion, as full watches divided by views:

- Post A is 3,200 / 8,000 = 0.40, or 40 percent.
- Post B is 4,000 / 20,000 = 0.20, or 20 percent.
- Post C is 2,500 / 5,000 = 0.50, or 50 percent.

Share rate, as shares divided by views:

- Post A is 160 / 8,000 = 0.02, or 2 percent.
- Post B is 100 / 20,000 = 0.005, or 0.5 percent.
- Post C is 200 / 5,000 = 0.04, or 4 percent.

Follows per 1,000 views, so the smaller post is comparable:

- Post A is 40 / 8 = 5.
- Post B is 15 / 20 = 0.75.
- Post C is 60 / 5 = 12.

Post B has the most views and the weakest rates. Post C has the fewest views and the strongest completion, share rate, and follows per 1,000 views. TikTok analytics, used this way, says to study C, not to celebrate B. If the product will not attribute follows to one video, do not pretend the last column is official. Note how followers moved on the days you posted and treat that note as a hint.

Rewatches fit the same table. Illustrative only: Post C has 400 rewatches on 5,000 views, which is 400 / 5,000 = 0.08, or 8 percent. Post B has 200 rewatches on 20,000 views, which is 200 / 20,000 = 0.01, or 1 percent. The view ranking and the rewatch ranking disagree. Believe the disagreement. It is the useful part.

Signal-to-effort is the same idea with hours attached. Illustrative effort: Post C took 2 hours and Post B took 6. Follows per hour are 60 / 2 = 30 for C and 15 / 6 = 2.5 for B. You would rather repeat C even if B looked like the winner in a view sort. Change the hours to your real edit time. Do not adopt 30 or 2.5 as a goal.

## How should a 7-day review turn into the next video?

A weekly review of TikTok analytics is a decision loop, not a report. The window in this workflow is the last 7 days. Longer windows hide what the last batch did. Shorter windows panic over one afternoon.

1. Export or write down every post from those 7 days. Include views, a completion figure, rewatches if shown, shares, comments worth reading, and follower movement you will stand behind.
2. Sort once by views and once by completion. Circle posts that fall when you change the sort. Those are the vanity spikes.
3. For the top two posts on completion or shares, write three lines: topic, opening line, edit pattern. Edit pattern means the shape of the cut. A demo before the explanation is one shape. A cut every time the claim changes is another. On-screen text that matches the first spoken sentence is a third.
4. For the bottom two, write which of those three lines failed. Do not write "the algorithm." That sentence does not change the next shoot.
5. Write one rule for the next batch. One rule is enough. Example shape: "Open with the result, then show the step that produced it," but only if that is what the strong posts did.
6. Film the next batch to that rule. Review it after 7 days. Retire the rule if the new posts do not beat your own recent completion or share rate.

The goal is better uploads, not a cleaner dashboard. If the review does not name the next opening line, it was a report.

Ask which topic, opening line, and edit pattern produced the strongest signal for the effort. Do not ask which video got the most views. Views stay in the table so you can compute rates. They do not pick the winner by themselves.

The opening is where most of the lesson sits. [The 3-second rule](/resources/the-3-second-rule-a-hook-taxonomy) is a taxonomy for what that opening can be. TikTok analytics tells you whether your version held people. It does not name the pattern until you watch the video again and write the pattern down. Pair the number with the tape. A high completion score on a video you cannot describe is a number you cannot repeat.

## How do audience and topic signals affect the next idea?

Audience quality changes the brief even when attention looks fine. Read comments for the nouns people use. If you posted about a beginner mistake and the comments are from experts correcting edge cases, the video may have reached a neighboring audience. The fix is not "post more." The fix is a clearer subject in the first line so classification and human expectation match.

Saves and follows are quality signals when they show up around the right subject. A save suggests the viewer wanted the video later. A follow suggests they wanted the account, not only the clip. A buy is the commercial version of the same question, and TikTok analytics will not always show the purchase. If you sell elsewhere, match the post time to downstream orders in your own records. Do not invent a TikTok conversion rate the screen does not show.

Search is the indirect effect. Better retention and a clearer topic help TikTok classify the video. Classification is what lets later viewers with that interest find it. Stuffing a caption with extra phrases does not repair a video people leave. When you change a topic, change the spoken subject and the visuals, not only the caption. Then watch whether completion on the new topic holds up against your own recent posts.

Compare TikTok with other short-form analytics only after each platform's definitions are explicit. [Instagram Reels vs YouTube Shorts analytics](/blog/instagram-reels-vs-youtube-shorts-analytics) is the place for that comparison. A Reels completion figure and a TikTok completion figure are cousins, not twins. Do not average them into one score for the week.

## What can TikTok analytics not decide?

TikTok analytics cannot show a competitor's completion, rewatches, or audience breakdown. If a planning meeting needs that, you are in public research, not in your analytics tab. Watch the competitor's opening, caption, and visible response, and say plainly that you cannot see their retention. ContHunt is for that public pass across TikTok, Instagram Reels, and YouTube Shorts. It is not a second copy of TikTok analytics.

TikTok analytics also cannot tell you the effort a video took or the margin on the product. It cannot tell you whether a format will work in a paid ad. Add those from your own notes. A high-share video that took a week and cannot be produced again is a weak template. A modest video you can repeat every Tuesday is a stronger operating decision.

Do not use a single outlier as the strategy. If one post in the illustrative table had been a news event you do not control, its views would be a spike, not a format. Mark outliers before you average. An average that includes one accident will bully the next month of ideas.

Likes-only reviews fail in a specific way. The team optimizes for a tap that barely interrupts the viewer, then cannot explain why follows did not move. Put likes in the sheet. Do not chair the meeting with them.

Posting-time debates fail the same way when they skip attention. A preferred hour cannot rescue an opening people leave. Check completion first. If completion is healthy and reach is narrow, then a distribution question, including timing, is worth a test. If completion is the weak column, timing is a distraction.

## What should the weekly sheet contain?

Keep one sheet. Add a row per post. Leave a cell blank when TikTok did not show the metric. A blank is not a zero.

| Column | What you write | How you use it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Date and topic | The subject in the creator's words | Groups repeats |
| Opening line | The first spoken or on-screen sentence | The thing you will reuse or drop |
| Edit pattern | The shape of the cut, in one phrase | Separates topic luck from structure |
| Views | The count TikTok shows | Denominator only |
| Completion | The stay figure your screen gives you | First sort |
| Rewatches | If shown | Second sort |
| Shares | The send count | Spread, separate from likes |
| Audience note | Who commented or followed, in words | Catches the wrong crowd |
| Effort | Hours, from you, not from TikTok | Signal-to-effort |
| Next rule | One sentence | The only required output. |

Review the sheet every 7 days. Compare top and bottom posts on completion and shares before you look at views. Write the rule. Archive the rule when the next batch disagrees with it.

The table above is an illustrative decision, restated as a rule rather than a new metric. The next videos copy Post C's opening and edit. In the illustration, 50 percent completion and a 4 percent share rate beat Post B's 20 percent completion and 0.5 percent share rate. C also produced more follows per hour of effort. Your rule must cite your rows. If you cannot point at two posts, you are not done reviewing.

TikTok analytics matters when it changes the next upload. Sort the last 7 days by completion, rewatches, and shares. Name the pattern those posts share. Leave the biggest view count in the denominator where it belongs. When the question moves from your account to the public feed, search that question.
