The YouTube Shorts algorithm in 2026 is best treated as a recommendation system that tests whether the right viewers keep watching and understand the topic. Written by the ContHunt team. It is not a retention percentage you can clear to unlock distribution. The YouTube Help pages this guide uses do not establish a universal half-second or three-second rule, and they do not publish a retention floor. Title, thumbnail, and description matter more for discovery than hidden tags. Tags play a minimal role except when a word is commonly misspelled. Hashtags are different: a hashtag in the title or description connects the video to hashtag search. The working loop is one niche promise and an opening that makes that promise obvious. Then review viewed-versus-swiped-away, retention, and search terms on your own channel.

## What YouTube's own pages say about being found

YouTube's help on [tags](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/146402?hl=en) is the clearest ranking of metadata, and it is easy to overread. Tags are descriptive keywords you can add so viewers can find a video. The same page says the title, thumbnail, and description are more important, because those are what viewers use to decide what to watch. Tags can help when the subject is commonly misspelled. Otherwise they play a minimal role in discovery. Stuffing tags into the description is called out as a problem: excessive tags in the description go against YouTube's spam, deceptive-practices, and scams policies. A Shorts strategy that starts with a tag list is starting at the part YouTube says matters least.

[Descriptions](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/12948449?hl=en) are doing a different job. YouTube says a description tells viewers and the system what the video is about. The first lines are what people see before they tap "Show more" on desktop or "more" on mobile. The description field allows a maximum of 5,000 characters. YouTube recommends a unique description for each video. It also recommends picking one or two main words, then featuring those words in the description and in the title. The Research tab in YouTube Analytics is one place it points you for popular phrases and synonyms. A keyword tool such as Google Ads Keyword Planner is the other. Search terms are matched with the video and with the text in the description. That is a reason to write a true description. It is not a reason to paste a paragraph of synonyms.

[Hashtags](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/10806146?hl=en) are keywords with a # in front of them. YouTube describes two ways to find videos with them. Type the hashtag in the search bar. Or select a hashtag in the title, below the title, or in the description. The results page shows videos that include that hashtag in the title or the description. A hashtag is therefore a search label, not a hidden boost. The sources behind this page do not say that adding hashtags guarantees recommendation. Use a few that name the topic. Do not use them as a substitute for a clear title.

| Signal | What the linked YouTube Help page supports | What it does not support |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Title, thumbnail, description | More important than tags; viewers use them to choose | A promise that any particular wording will be recommended |
| One or two main words | Put them in the title and prominently in the description | A long synonym block |
| Description length | Maximum of 5,000 characters | A reason to fill all 5,000 |
| Tags | Minimal discovery role; useful for common misspellings | Tag stuffing, including in the description |
| Hashtags | Hashtag search, when the tag is in the title or description | A guaranteed recommendation lift. |

Hidden tags and hashtags are not the same field. Tags are added in YouTube Studio, under more options on a new upload or "show more" on a video you already published. Hashtags are typed with a # in the title or description, where viewers can tap them. If a tool talks about "tags" while you are looking at a caption full of hashtags, it is mixing two systems. [YouTube Shorts hashtags](/blog/youtube-shorts-hashtags-guide) goes further on placement. This section only needs the split. Tags are the weak, mostly invisible field. Hashtags are a public search label. The title, the opening frame, and the first lines of the description carry the promise.

## How to write the description so the topic is classifiable

A classifiable Short has a title, a first frame, and a description that agree. YouTube's description guidance is a procedure, not a vibe.

1. Write one sentence that says what the video is about, using the one or two main words. Put that sentence in the first lines, because that is the part viewers see before they expand the description.
2. Keep the description unique to this video. A default description can hold the channel boilerplate that YouTube allows you to reuse, but the part about this video has to change. A repeated block makes similar uploads harder to tell apart in search.
3. Add the extra channel information after the video-specific lines, not before them. Credits, a related video, and profile links belong in that second half if you need them.
4. Format only to make the text scannable. YouTube's help says the description can be bold, italic, or strikethrough, and that a line starting with `*`, `+`, or `-` followed by a space becomes a bullet. Formatting is not a ranking trick.
5. Check the description the way a stranger will see it: in search, on the watch page, and on a phone. If the main words are below the fold, they are not doing the job YouTube told you the first lines do.

Pretend you are searching for the video. The words you would type are the words that belong near the start, if they are honest. If you would type a phrase the footage never shows, leave it out. Mismatched language is how a Short gets found by the wrong query and then loses the viewer. You will see that later as a search term that does not fit the retention graph.

The thumbnail, for a Short, is often a frame from the video rather than a designed long-form image. Treat the opening frame as the thumbnail's job: the niche and the payoff or the problem have to be readable before someone scrolls. A title that says "charger keeps falling" and a first frame of a tidy desk are a classification error, even if both are attractive.

## Viewed versus swiped away is a diagnostic

When a Short appears in a feed surface, the viewer either keeps watching or swipes away. Viewed-versus-swiped-away is the diagnostic for that moment. It tells you whether the opening promise held a viewer who was already being shown the video. It does not tell you that a particular percentage is a pass, and YouTube does not publish a universal line in the sources used here. Compare the figure with your own similar Shorts, on the same topic and a similar length. A tutorial and a joke are not a pair. A 15-second Short and a much longer one are not a pair.

Read it beside the rest of the channel's evidence: average view duration, rewatches, likes, comments, and the search terms YouTube Analytics shows for that video. One high swipe-away rate on a mismatched topic is a targeting or packaging clue. The same rate on a video that search terms say is reaching the right query is an opening clue. Those are different repairs. Hashtags are the wrong first repair in both cases.

Where the cards sit in Studio, and how views differ from engaged views after YouTube's counting change, is the subject of [YouTube Shorts analytics](/blog/youtube-shorts-analytics). This page uses the metric as a creative check. It does not restate every definition.

## A review workflow that is not YouTube's pipeline

The sequence below is an editorial test. It is not a description of YouTube's internal distribution stages. Nobody outside YouTube can see those stages, and inventing names for them does not make the next upload clearer.

1. Opening. Watch the first frame with the sound off and ask whether a stranger can state the promise. If they cannot, the swipe metric will be hard to interpret because the viewer never received an offer.
2. Viewing. After the upload has had time to gather a real sample, read retention and engagement together with the topic and the length. A spike can be a rewatch of a confusing step or a rewatch of a satisfying one. Look at the frame before you celebrate it.
3. Comparison. Put the Short next to your own recent uploads of similar length and topic. A video that loses to your own median is a clearer lesson than a video that loses to a stranger's viral hit.
4. Search. Read the search terms that actually surfaced the video. If they are the one or two words you wrote, the packaging and the query agree. If they are unrelated, fix the description and the title toward the video you made, not toward the accidental query.

Write the four notes in the same place every time. A workflow you do not record becomes a feeling that the algorithm changed. Often the opening changed, or the topic drifted, or you started comparing a new format with an old one.

## Five hook formats to test, none of them a ranking factor

A hook here is the opening that makes the niche promise obvious. It has to be obvious enough for a viewer to stay. The five formats below are creative options. They are not features YouTube has published as ranking inputs, and they are not a promise that any of them will travel. [The 3-second rule](/resources/the-3-second-rule-a-hook-taxonomy) is a wider pattern library for short-form openings. Use it as a menu. Do not import a three-second law into YouTube from it. This page's YouTube sources do not establish that law.

| Hook | What the viewer gets immediately | Skip it when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Final result first | The finished outcome, before the build-up | The result is meaningless until the story explains it |
| Belief challenge | A claim the niche treats as settled, then the real comparison | You cannot show the comparison, only the slogan |
| Visual pattern interrupt | A close-up or a composition break, with the subject still readable | The clever frame hides what the video is about |
| Negative hook | A specific warning or common failure, then the correction | The warning is exaggerated or the video never delivers the fix |
| Curiosity gap | A concrete question the video will close | The payoff is vaguer than the question. |

The final-result opening works because it proves there is a payoff. "Here is the desk after" before "here is the mess" gives a viewer a reason to care about the steps. It fails when the after shot could belong to any video in the niche. Specificity is the proof, not the fact that something looks finished.

The belief challenge is the format behind an illustrative line such as "I changed the opening without changing the topic. Here is what happened." The line is only fair if you show the comparison and its limits. One channel, one topic, two openings, is an anecdote until your own analytics repeat the pattern. Do not narrate it as a universal law. That is how a honest test becomes a false hook, which is the failure mode of this format.

A visual pattern interrupt is a change in composition, not a pile of effects. A close-up of the broken part, a hand entering a still frame, or a cut from the product to the failure can interrupt the pattern. The viewer should still know the subject. If you need a caption to explain what the frame is, the interrupt was noise.

The negative hook starts with a warning or a common failure. The illustrative shape is "Stop doing this if you want [Result]." Use it only when the mistake is real and the video gives the correction. A fake emergency trains the viewer to swipe the next time you use that tone. Test it against a plain descriptive opening on a similar topic before you make it the channel's voice.

The curiosity gap is a question that the footage can close. "The one mistake I made..." is the shape, and it is empty if the mistake is never named. Compare a question opening with a descriptive opening on two similar Shorts. Keep whichever one your retention and your swipe diagnostic support. Do not keep the question because it felt clever in the edit.

Whichever format you pick, the video has to deliver the benefit the opening named. A hook that the footage betrays will show up as an early stay followed by a drop at the moment the promise was due. That pattern is a writing problem, not a metadata problem.

## Point the viewer at a real next watch

A Short can support a longer channel relationship when the next video continues the same promise. A related video or a playlist is appropriate when it is actually the next chapter. That chapter is the mess, then the fix, then the mistake people make on the fix. It is not appropriate as a generic "subscribe and watch everything" gesture.

The practical goal is viewer satisfaction and more total watch time from people who wanted this topic. It is not a guaranteed distribution unlock. If the next video is unrelated, the link teaches the wrong viewers to click and teaches you nothing. Leave it off until you have a true sequel.

## What to check first when Shorts stop growing

Start with behavior, not with hashtags. The order is the order of the evidence.

1. Did viewers keep watching or swipe away, relative to your own similar Shorts?
2. Does the title state the niche and the promise in plain language?
3. Does the opening frame show the payoff or the problem? Or does it show a logo, a face waiting to talk, or a result from a different video?
4. Are the one or two main words in the first lines of the description, and is the description unique?
5. Do the search terms in Analytics match that promise? If YouTube is matching the Short to a topic you did not make, rewrite the packaging toward the footage. If there are almost no search terms and the feed is the source, the opening is the first place to work, not the tag box.
6. Only then look at hashtags, and only to see whether they name the same topic. Adding more will not repair a swipe problem.

Also check whether you changed more than one variable. A new topic, a new opening, and a new length on the same day cannot tell you which change the viewers responded to. The next upload should isolate one.

## Research the opening before you film it

Public videos are a way to see which promises and openings a niche is already using. They are not a way to see a competitor's private retention. A high public view count does not reveal the swipe rate or the moment of the drop. ContHunt searches TikTok, Instagram Reels, and YouTube Shorts so the examples are not limited to the app you happened to scroll. Deep Search watches 500 to 1,000 videos in under five minutes and returns a ranked answer with the videos as evidence. The rank fits the question you asked. It is not a measurement of how long strangers watched. Tracking in ContHunt is experimental, so do not treat a tracked list as the algorithm.

An illustrative test log, not a result, keeps one topic. The topic stays the charger falls off the desk. Version A opens with the words here are three desk fixes. Version B opens on the cable pulling the charger, with the problem readable in the frame. You publish them as separate Shorts. You do not change the title's topic between them. You read viewed-versus-swiped-away, the retention graph, and the search terms for each. You keep the opening your own baseline supports. You do not average either Short with a long-form upload. You do not declare a winner from public likes on someone else's version of the same idea.

Record the variable in a sentence before you publish. "Only the opening changed." If you cannot write that sentence, you are not testing a hook. You are posting.

The algorithm, stated as something you can operate, is that feedback loop. Clear promise, readable packaging, an opening people can accept or reject immediately, and an honest read of your own analytics. Secret metadata is the smaller lever, and YouTube's tag documentation already says so.
