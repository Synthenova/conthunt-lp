A market trend, in this guide, is a change in what people make and respond to that is speeding up, not a topic that is merely large. Identifying one starts in public short-form video, not in a quarterly report that describes the shift after it is obvious. Watch TikTok, Reels, and YouTube Shorts for a keyword, a sound, a format, or a complaint whose use is accelerating. A flat topic with a huge volume is yesterday's subject. A smaller topic that is clearly spreading is the one to study. Audio often moves before the pictures have a name. Test the signal with a few videos before you rewrite a product plan, and read the questions competitors leave unanswered.

## How is social intelligence different from social listening?

Social intelligence is different from social listening because listening tells you what already happened, and intelligence asks what is speeding up. A listening report that ranks topics by how often they were mentioned is a rear-view mirror.

It is useful. It is not a trend call. A topic mentioned constantly, with no change in pace, is a fixture. You already know it. A topic mentioned less often, but in more videos this week than last week, across more than one creator, is a candidate.

This guide calls that pace social velocity: the rate at which a keyword, a format, or a sound is spreading across short-form platforms. Volume is the count. Velocity is the change in the count. You want the change. A large, flat count feels important in a dashboard because the number is big. The change is what tells you the audience is moving.

An illustrative contrast, not a measurement from a market, makes the arithmetic obvious. Suppose phrase A shows up in 100 videos and is growing at 50 percent a day, and phrase B shows up in 1,000 videos and is flat. On the next day, phrase A would be at 100 × 1.5 = 150. On the day after, 150 × 1.5 = 225. Phrase B is still at 1,000. After two days of that pace, A is still smaller than B, and A is the one you study. The raw leaderboard would have told you to study B. The leaderboard is listening. The day-over-day change is the intelligence. Do not treat 50 percent as a threshold you must hit in the real world. The point of the illustration is the direction. A real candidate can grow more slowly than that and still matter, if the direction holds across several days and across creators who do not work for the same brand.

Count creators, not just mentions. Ten videos from one brand is a campaign. Ten videos from ten unrelated people is the start of a behavior. A trend you can act on has to survive that split. If you remove the brand's own accounts and the phrase disappears, you were watching an ad, not a market.

## Why does the audio often move first?

The audio often moves first because a sound is reusable before a category has agreed on the pictures. On TikTok and on YouTube Shorts in particular, creators borrow audio the way they borrow a sentence structure.

The same sound under three different kitchens, three different desks, or three different routines is a format being born. The objects in frame may still look unrelated. The sound is the thread.

When you review a niche, write the sound down beside the topic, not as a footnote. Note whether the audio is a song, a voiceover line, or an original noise from one video that others are now copying. Then ask what the sound is doing. Is it a punchline that requires a specific cut? Is it a narration bed people use because it feels calm? Is it a challenge line that forces a before-and-after? The job of the sound tells you the job of the video you would have to make. "Use the trending audio" is not a brief. "Use this audio because it sets up a three-beat correction, and our product is the third beat" is a brief.

A sound that is everywhere outside your niche is not automatically early inside it. The useful version is the sound that has entered your niche in the last few days, on accounts that usually talk about your subject. That is an early format. A sound that has been under every niche for a month is a costume. You can still wear it. Do not call it a market signal. Call it packaging, and judge it with the packaging tests you already trust.

If you cannot name what the sound is doing in one sentence, you do not understand the trend yet. Watch three videos that use it, with the sound on, and write the sentence. The sentence is the brief. The audio file is not.

## How do you map a trend across industries?

Map a trend across industries by asking which neighboring category has not made the video yet, once a look or a behavior is already crowded in the category where it started. People do not keep their taste in a single aisle.

A way of filming, a palette, or a standard of "clean" that saturates skincare can show up next in home goods, in the clothes on the same person, or in the room behind them. The source example this guide keeps is the "clean girl" look: a skincare aesthetic that is also a home aesthetic, once you notice the surfaces, the light, and the absence of clutter. The product changes. The standard the viewer is applying does not.

Use the map as a hypothesis generator, not as a law. The steps are plain.

1. Name the saturated behavior in the original category, in words a creator would say.
2. List two adjacent categories where the same person spends money. Not a random industry. The same person.
3. Search those adjacent categories for the behavior. If it is already crowded there too, you are late. If it is missing, you may be early.
4. Check whether the adjacent viewer wants the behavior, or only the original viewer wanted it. A look that depends on a bathroom mirror may not survive in a garage. The absence of videos is sometimes a lack of demand, not a lack of competition.

The third and fourth steps are where teams lie to themselves. An empty category feels like a blue ocean. Sometimes the ocean is empty because the fish are not there. Before you move a budget, read the comments under the few videos that do exist at the boundary. Are people asking for the product in the new setting, or ignoring it? Asking is a signal. Silence is a warning. One enthusiastic comment is not a market. The same request, worded by different people under different videos, is closer.

Write the hypothesis in a sentence you can falsify. "People who already film a simplified morning skincare routine are starting to film the shelf and the counter, and nobody in compact storage is showing a counter that meets that standard." That sentence can be wrong, which is why it is useful. "The clean look is the future of retail" cannot be wrong in a way that teaches you anything. Prefer the sentence you can retire on Friday.

## How do you read sentiment without inventing a market?

Read sentiment by collecting the same complaint across many videos, and ignore a single angry thread. A shift in how people talk about a competitor is a market gap only when it repeats.

"I hate the lid" under one video is a person. "The lid leaks" under a dozen videos, from people who are not talking to each other, is a design fact the market is already narrating. Your product either solves that fact or it does not. The trend is the narration, and the narration is early product research if you write down the noun they keep using.

Do this on public video, not on a score that pretends to be a survey.

- Search the competitor's name and the product's name across TikTok, Reels, and Shorts.
- Pull the videos where a person is demonstrating, not where the brand is announcing.
- Read the comments for a repeated noun: a part, a step, a price feeling, a comparison to an older version.
- Keep a tally by hand for a small set. You are looking for repetition, not for a sentiment percentage you would not be able to defend.

Ask which complaints show up when people demonstrate this kind of product, and which videos actually show the failure rather than just saying the brand name. Then open the evidence. A ranked paragraph without the videos is a rumor. The videos are the source. A score in that result judges the fit of the video to your question. It does not measure how many customers will switch, and it does not replace a support queue or a sales call. It tells you what the public record is already saying, while there is still time to decide whether you believe it.

Do not treat a tracked view count as the size of the market. View counts are not revenue. They are a reason to go read the comments.

## How do you test a trend with content before you bet the product?

Test a trend with content by publishing a few small pieces that each make a different promise, then reading which promise people keep. This guide's version is three hooks about the same possible offer, posted as short-form videos, with everything else held as steady as you can hold it.

The hook that people save is the one with the strongest commercial hint, because a save is a person saying they want the idea again later. Saves are read in the platform's own analytics for the account that posted. They are not a number a public search can honestly invent for you.

The three hooks should differ in the claim, not in the production value. If one video is beautiful and two are rough, you tested editing, not the trend. An illustrative set for a compact coffee maker, labeled as a test design rather than a result:

- "The step everyone skips before the first brew."
- "What I stopped buying once this lived on the counter."
- "The mess this makes that nobody films."

Same product, same length band, same week if you can. After they have had a fair window, compare saves and completion on your account. The hook people save is the promise to expand. The hook people finish but do not save was entertaining and may not be a product. The hook people do not finish was not the trend you hoped. Do not "average" them into a maybe. Pick one and make the next three videos about that one promise.

Only after the content test should a product change enter the conversation. A roadmap rewrite based on one weekend of videos is how teams chase a sound. A roadmap note that says "we saw this request repeat, we posted three promises, and the save clustered on the mess claim, so the next prototype addresses the mess" is a trend process. The size of the bet should match the size of the evidence. A repeated comment plus a saved hook is a reason to prototype. It is not, by itself, a reason to retool a factory. Say that out loud in the meeting. The meeting will try to skip ahead.

## Where should you look when competitors are not looking?

Look where competitors are not looking when the crowded place is already priced into their content and the quiet place still has a buyer talking. Two quiet places show up often.

The first is the comment they do not answer. The second is the platform they do not publish on. Both can be opportunities. Both can be traps. The work is telling them apart.

Unanswered questions are the cleaner signal. Read the comments on the videos where your competitors look most successful. The questions that appear more than once, and that the creator never answers in a later video, are a brief they have declined. You can take the brief. Make the video that answers it, in your product's language, without sneering at the other channel. The viewer does not care about the rivalry. They care about the lid, the sizing, the setup, the exception. Answer that.

A platform gap is the messier signal. A competitor who posts only on one of TikTok, Reels, or Shorts may have left attention on the table, or they may have learned that the other platforms do not hold their buyer. Before you call the empty platform a blue ocean, check whether anyone in the category succeeds there. If the whole category is absent, ask why before you volunteer to be the test. If peers are present and this one competitor is not, the gap is more interesting. You are not educating a platform from zero. You are showing up where the buyer already watches, in a slot this rival has left open.

Ghosting the competitor, in the sense this guide uses, means mapping their absence. It does not mean pretending they are not good at the thing they are good at. Note what they cover every week. Then note the question they never cover. The trend is often in the second list, because the first list is the consensus. Consensus is comfortable and late. The unanswered question is uncomfortable and, sometimes, early. Put both lists on one page so the absence is visible next to the presence. An absence you cannot see will not make it into the plan.

## What cadence keeps a trend process honest?

An honest trend process runs on three clocks: a daily pulse, a weekly cross-check, and a monthly decision. Daily is for noticing.

Weekly is for refusing false alarms. Monthly is the only clock allowed to change a roadmap. Teams that change the roadmap daily are not fast. They are loud. Teams that look once a quarter are not careful. They are late. The three clocks are the compromise this guide actually recommends.

Daily, look at velocity rather than at a highlight reel. Which keyword, sound, or format in your subject is spreading today that was smaller recently? A handful of candidates is enough. You are not writing the report in the morning. You are catching the phrase that deserves a seat in Friday's review. If a day produces nothing, write "nothing" down. A process that cannot record a null day will invent a trend to justify the meeting.

Weekly, take up to three of those candidates and cross them with search demand. Social video can run ahead of search, and it can also run ahead of nothing. A phrase that is spreading in Shorts and also rising as a search is stronger than a phrase that only exists inside one sound. This guide's weekly step is that cross-check: three social signals, set next to a search-trend reading, with a note on whether search agrees, lags, or contradicts. A tool such as Glimpse is one way teams read the search side. The social side is the videos. A weekly routine that sorts formats, sounds, topics, and products into emerging, rising, peaking, and fading is written up in [Build a weekly trend radar for your niche](/resources/build-a-weekly-trend-radar-for-your-niche). Use that page for the Friday worksheet. Use this page for the decision the worksheet is supposed to earn.

Monthly, change the content plan and, if the evidence survived, the product plan. The input is the velocity leaders that made it through a month of weekly checks, plus the content test if you ran one. A candidate that spiked for three days and then vanished does not get a roadmap line. A candidate that showed up in week one, was still spreading in week three, and produced a save when you tested the hook, does. Write the change as a sentence with a date. "In March we prototype a quieter lid, because the leak complaint repeated all February and the save sat on the mess hook." Dated sentences are how a trend process stays honest. Undated enthusiasm is how it turns into a mood.

## What should you refuse to call a trend?

Refuse to call it a trend when the evidence is a single spike, a calendar, or your own campaign. The word "trend" is expensive.

Spent on the wrong object, it sends a team to make a product for an audience of one afternoon.

Do not promote these to a trend without more evidence:

- One video with a large view count. That is a hit. A hit can start a trend, and it often does not. Wait to see whether other creators pick up the structure.
- Your own ad, or a creator you paid. That is distribution you bought. Remove paid posts before you count.
- A holiday, a season, or a sports calendar. Recurrence is not acceleration. Plan for it as a calendar, which you already know how to do.
- A celebrity post that nobody imitates. Influence without imitation is a broadcast, not a behavior.
- A topic that is large and flat. That is the category you are already in. Keep serving it. Do not hold a kickoff about it.

The positive tests are the ones already in this guide. More than one unrelated creator. A pace that is up, not only a total that is high. A sound or a format you can describe in a sentence. A comment that repeats. A content test you are willing to lose. If a candidate fails those and the room still wants to call it a trend, the room is bored. Boredom is not a market signal. Ship the work you already decided, and let the daily pulse keep running until something actually moves.

## How do you write the search that finds a candidate?

Write the search as a question with a subject, a window, and a decision attached, not as a keyword you hope will inspire you. "Coffee" is not a question.

"Which formats around compact coffee makers are spreading on TikTok, Reels, and Shorts right now, and which of them mention a mess or a cleanup?" is a question. The second version tells the search what to count as relevant and tells you what you will do with the answer. You will look at mess and cleanup. You will ignore a pretty pour that does not bear on the decision.

If the plan is searching the wrong words, the velocity you get back will be the velocity of the wrong conversation. A minute with the plan saves a week of arguing about a chart.

When the answer arrives, force it into a one-page note. A note is what makes the monthly meeting short. Without the note, everyone rewatches their favorite video and calls that research.

| Field | What you write | Illustrative example, not a finding |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Signal | The phrase, sound, or format | Counter cleanup after a compact brew |
| Where | Platforms and whether brands were excluded | Reels and Shorts, brand accounts removed |
| Pace | Up, flat, or fading, in words | Up across the last two weekly checks |
| Adjacent | The next category, if any | Counter storage, not another coffee flavor |
| Test | The hook you will actually post | "The mess nobody films" |
| Decision date | When you will keep or kill it | The last Friday of the month |

The illustrative row is a way to see the fields. It is not a claim about coffee makers. Replace every cell with what you saw, including the cell that says "flat" or "kill." A note that can only record good news is a marketing asset. You needed a research asset.

The wider name for this habit, if you need it for a team that does not speak in velocity, is content intelligence: using public content as evidence about a market, with the videos kept attached to the claim. The definition and the boundaries are in [What is content intelligence?](/blog/what-is-content-intelligence). The boundary matters. Public video can show you what people demonstrate and what they ask. It cannot show you your competitor's margin, and it cannot show you a private retention curve. Stay inside what the videos can support. When the note says "we think," point at the video that made you think it. If you cannot point, delete the sentence. A trend process that allows unpointed sentences will fill the roadmap with them, and the roadmap will not survive contact with a buyer.

## Market trend checklist

Use this market trend checklist before a candidate is allowed to change a plan. A candidate that fails a row stays a note, not a roadmap line.

- The candidate is defined as a change in pace, not as a large flat topic.
- Brand and paid videos were removed before the count was treated as behavior.
- More than one unrelated creator is involved, or the candidate is marked as unconfirmed.
- The sound, if there is one, has a job you can describe in a sentence.
- An adjacent category was checked for absence versus lack of demand.
- Repeated viewer language was copied down, not summarized into a mood.
- Up to three weekly candidates were set next to search demand.
- A content test used more than one hook, and saves were read on the posting account.
- The roadmap changes at most monthly, in a dated sentence, and only for a candidate that survived.
