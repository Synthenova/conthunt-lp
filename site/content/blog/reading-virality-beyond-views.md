Reading virality beyond views means ranking a short-form video by outlier ratio, velocity, share rate, save rate and comment intent. Written by the ContHunt team. Rank that way instead of by the raw view total. Views are the first number everyone checks and the least comparable one. The same count can mean a video people chose to watch. It can mean a video that was shown widely and ignored. It can also mean a creator whose audience always shows up.

## Why a view count is a weak yardstick.

A view count is a weak yardstick because platforms define a view differently, change that definition, and rank on actions rather than on account size. YouTube states that since 31 March 2025, Shorts views count each time a Short starts to play or replay, with no minimum watch time. The previous measure still exists in analytics as "engaged views" ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/10059070)). A Shorts view count today and a TikTok view count are not the same thing. A Shorts view count from 2024 is not the same as one from 2026.

Platforms also say they rank on actions, not on size. TikTok's [recommendation explainer](https://newsroom.tiktok.com/en-us/how-tiktok-recommends-videos-for-you/) lists user interactions, video information, and device and account settings as inputs. It gives a strong indicator, like finishing a longer video, more weight than a weak one. It says follower count and past high performers are not direct factors. Instagram's [ranking explainer](https://about.instagram.com/blog/announcements/instagram-ranking-explained) says Reels ranking predicts whether a viewer will watch a reel all the way through, like it or reshare it.

If ranking rewards completion and sharing, those are the signals worth reading from outside. You cannot see completion, but you can see its shadows.

## Five ratios that travel better than views.

Five ratios travel better than a view total: outlier ratio, velocity, share rate, save rate and comment intent, and each one answers a different question.

| Metric | Formula | What it answers | Watch-out |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Outlier ratio | Views ÷ creator's median views on recent posts | Did this video beat its own account's norm? | Needs 10+ recent posts to be stable. |
| Velocity | Views gained in first 24 hours, or views per hour since posting | Is it spreading fast right now? | Needs timestamps or daily snapshots |
| Share rate | Shares ÷ views | Did people pass it on? | Some platforms hide or rename shares and sends |
| Save rate | Saves ÷ views | Is it useful enough to keep? | Strongest for tutorials, recipes, products |
| Comment intent | Share of comments by type (see below) | What did the audience want to do? | Needs manual or analyzed sampling. |

A sixth, reach beyond followers (views ÷ follower count), is a rough guide to whether a video escaped the creator's own audience. Use it as a screen, not a score.

Each ratio has a different job. The outlier ratio finds the interesting videos. Velocity tells you how current the interest is. Share and save rates tell you why people cared. Comment intent tells you what they wanted next.

## A worked example (illustrative numbers)

The worked example compares three illustrative videos so the ratios can overrule the view ranking. All numbers are invented to show the method.

| | Video A | Video B | Video C |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Views | 1,200,000 | 180,000 | 640,000 |
| Creator's median views | 400,000 | 12,000 | 500,000 |
| Shares | 1,200 | 5,400 | 640 |
| Saves | 2,400 | 7,200 | 1,000 |
| Outlier ratio | 3.0 | 15.0 | 1.3 |
| Share rate | 0.1% | 3.0% | 0.1% |
| Save rate | 0.2% | 4.0% | 0.16%. |

Video A has the most views, and it is a good result for its creator. Video C has a decent total and barely moved against its own norm. Video B has a seventh of A's views, but it ran 15 times its creator's median, and one in 33 viewers shared it. If you could study one video, B is the one. It found an audience beyond the creator's own, and people sent it on.

Cutoffs depend on the niche, so build your own baseline. Pull 100 videos in your niche, compute share rate for each, and use the top quartile's range as your bar.

## Reading comment intent.

Comment intent is the share of comments by type. It shows what the audience wanted to do next rather than how loud the view count was. Comments are the cheapest audience research you can run. Sort 100 comments per video into types.

| Type | Example | What it signals |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Purchase intent | "Link?", "where can I buy" | Demand with a missing path to buy |
| Question | "Does it work on thick hair?" | The next piece of content to make |
| Objection | "This would break in a week" | The proof beat you need |
| Share intent | "Sending this to my sister" | Strong shareability |
| Replay | "Watched this three times" | The hook or payoff holds attention |
| Joke or noise | Emoji strings | Little to read. |

Two videos with equal views can have very different mixes. A product video with 25 purchase-intent comments per 1,000 comments tells you more than one with 25 jokes.

## What public data cannot show.

Public data cannot show retention curves, average watch time, completion rate, audience demographics or sales, because those belong to the account owner. You cannot see them from outside. When you commission a creator, ask for a retention screenshot for the post you want to learn from. It is the one metric outsiders cannot infer.

ContHunt's own scores, including hook and relevance scores, come from analyzing the video and judging its fit to your question. They are evidence about the content and are not measured viewer retention. Use them to find and sort videos, and confirm what matters in your own analytics.

## Red flags before you copy a video.

Red flags are reasons to pause before you copy a video. Those reasons include a big view count with nothing behind it and generic comment floods. They also include a single-day spike and an outlier that is really a one-off format. Look at more than one before you decide.

- **Views with nothing behind them.** Big counts and almost no comments, shares or saves can mean paid amplification or low-quality traffic.
- **Generic comment floods.** Dozens of near-identical praise comments suggest an engagement pod or automation.
- **A single spike.** A video that shot up on one day and flatlined may have been pushed or reposted by a large account.
- **Missing context.** A video that looks like an outlier because the creator posted a rare format may not generalize.

Inflated numbers exist. The FTC's [final rule on fake reviews and testimonials](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/08/federal-trade-commission-announces-final-rule-banning-fake-reviews-testimonials) covers selling or buying fake social media indicators such as bot-generated followers or views. These red flags are heuristics, not proof, so look at more than one before you decide.

## A four-step triage.

The four-step triage keeps videos at 3x outlier ratio or more. It ranks those by share rate plus save rate. It reads comments on the top ten. It tags the hook and format of the top five. Work in that order.

1. **Filter** a relevant set by outlier ratio, and keep videos at 3x or more.
2. **Rank** the survivors by share rate plus save rate.
3. **Read** 50 comments on each of the top ten and classify them.
4. **Tag** the hook and format of the top five, using the [hook taxonomy](/resources/the-3-second-rule-a-hook-taxonomy).

## Decide when the five ratios disagree.

The five ratios will not always rank the same video first. Follow them in order instead of averaging them into one score. Outlier ratio picks the interesting videos. Velocity says whether that interest is current. Share rate and save rate say why people cared. Comment intent says what they wanted next. A blended number hides which of those jobs failed.

The illustrative trio already in the worked example is enough to see a disagreement. Video A has 1,200,000 views and an outlier ratio of 3.0. Its share rate is 0.1% and its save rate is 0.2%. Video B has 180,000 views, an outlier ratio of 15.0, a share rate of 3.0% and a save rate of 4.0%. Video C has 640,000 views and an outlier ratio of 1.3. Its share rate is 0.1% and its save rate is 0.16%. A view ranking puts A first, then C, then B. Outlier ratio puts B first, then A, then C. Share rate and save rate also put B first. The ratios agree with each other and disagree with the view ranking. Study B. The share line in the worked example is reproducible: 180,000 ÷ 5,400 = 33.3, which is the "one in 33 viewers shared it" claim. The "seventh of A's views" line is a description, not a cell to copy. The arithmetic is 180,000 ÷ 1,200,000 = 0.15, while 1 ÷ 7 is about 0.143.

Reproduce the ratios before you brief from a sheet. Outlier ratio is views divided by the creator's median. For A, 1,200,000 ÷ 400,000 = 3.0. For B, 180,000 ÷ 12,000 = 15.0. For C, 640,000 ÷ 500,000 = 1.28, which the table rounds to 1.3. Share rate is shares divided by views. For A, 1,200 ÷ 1,200,000 = 0.1%. For B, 5,400 ÷ 180,000 = 3.0%. For C, 640 ÷ 640,000 = 0.1%. Save rate is saves divided by views. For A, 2,400 ÷ 1,200,000 = 0.2%. For B, 7,200 ÷ 180,000 = 4.0%. For C, 1,000 ÷ 640,000 = 0.15625%, which the table shows as 0.16%. If your sheet does not reproduce the ratio, fix the sheet before you copy the opening.

When the ratios split, do not invent a tie-break weight. Apply the four-step triage. Keep videos at 3x or more on outlier ratio. That drops C at 1.3 and keeps A at 3.0 and B at 15.0. Rank the survivors by share rate plus save rate. A is 0.1% + 0.2% = 0.3%. B is 3.0% + 4.0% = 7.0%. B ranks ahead of A. Then read 50 comments on each video you might actually copy. Tag the hook and format of the few you will brief, using the [hook taxonomy](/resources/the-3-second-rule-a-hook-taxonomy). Velocity breaks a remaining tie only when you have timestamps or daily snapshots. A high outlier ratio on a video that has stopped gaining views is a lesson about an opening. It is not a reason to join something that has already peaked.

Two cautions stay in force. These cutoffs are not universal. Pull 100 videos in your niche, compute share rate for each, and use that top quartile as your bar. None of the ratios is a retention curve. Public counts still cannot show completion, demographics or sales. ContHunt scores, including hook and relevance scores, judge the content and its fit to your question. They are not measured viewer retention.
